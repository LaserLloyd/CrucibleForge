### 1
Steam rises off Wren's coat. Barnaby takes the dripping umbrella toward the peg rack, then thinks better of it and leaves it where she propped it. Every head at the table turns toward the head of the bench, and the stove ticks.

**Ottoline:** "Twenty-eight years, Inspector, not twenty. The tide has turned that many winters under my eye, and it has never once lied to me."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, sit down by the stove, Inspector, for you are wet through and the soup is hot."
**Ottoline:** "The lights were true. Ask any tide-gauge on this coast."
**Jory:** "Lights were lit. Cutter struck anyway."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the log says the High Light burns steady. The keeper signs what the log asks."
**Pip:** "I counted 5 lamps on the quay head that night, sir..."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, the boy counts everything when strangers come, and the bread goes cold while he does it."

Out past the window the harbour water slaps at the quay stones, black and restless, and the Stack is somewhere out there under it, covered by the making tide. A gust finds the door and the oilskins on their pegs sway.

### 2
Jory sets down his mug and looks at the stove, not at the notebook. The pause stretches out a little before he speaks.

**Jory:** "Fourteen years. Not ten."
**Jory:** "Left quay at nine. Cutter ran light."
**Jory:** "Wind from the west. Sea moderate."
**Jory:** "Pilot took the line at buoy. Held it. Boat struck the reef."
**Ottoline:** "He is the best pilot this tide has ever carried over the bar, Inspector. Write that down."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the pilot brings every cutter in clean. Everyone on this quay knows it."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, will anyone take bread while the pilot talks, for it is still warm?"
**Pip:** "There are 3 loaves left on the board, sir..."

Dilys looks very hard at the table and not at the pilot. Barnaby slides the bread board down the bench toward Wren. Outside, a halyard slaps against a mast somewhere along the quay, and the lamp over the table swings a hand's breadth on its chain and settles again.

The lamp over the table swings a hand's breadth on its chain and settles again, and the shadows on the stone floor slide with it. Rain ticks against the glass in little handfuls, and a thin draught from under the door stirs the hems of the oilskins on their pegs.

### 3
Barnaby wipes his hands down his apron before he has finished the question, and does not seem to notice that he does it. A gust leans on the shutters.

**Ottoline:** "The keeper trims and lights it, Inspector. Dilys climbs at every turn of the tide, wet or dry."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the harbour-master speaks of the old days. The knee is bad and the steps are many."
**Dilys:** "Mind, colza oil, never paraffin."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, I was in the scullery from the five o'clock bell until supper, with nine loaves, Inspector, and the oven door that would not shut, and I never looked up."
**Jory:** "Smelled the bread. From the quay."
**Pip:** "I carried 2 trays out to the cart, sir..."

Barnaby wipes his hands down his apron again and turns toward the hatch as if the soup needed him.

**Ottoline:** "The tide takes little interest in ovens, Inspector. Ask your next question."

Nobody reaches to close the shutter latch, and the candle on the sill gutters twice before it steadies and burns tall again.

Somewhere in the roof a single drip falls into a bucket, patient and regular, and every head lifts a little toward it before settling.

### 4
Dilys keeps her eyes on the lamp log in front of her, one finger flat on the page. The wind that has been rattling the shutters all evening drops for a moment.

**Dilys:** "Mind, the boy goes up at dusk. The keeper does not climb. The knee says it is too far."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the keeper signs the log. The log says trimmed, lit, true. The lamp burns steady from the window."
**Ottoline:** "The tide has little quarrel with an honest log, Inspector."
**Jory:** "Keeper tells it plainly."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, leave the lad be, Inspector, for he is only sixteen and the broth is going cold."

Pip keeps his right hand deep in his pocket and winds the end of his red scarf round two fingers of the other. A faint smell of lamp oil hangs about him.

**Pip:** "There is nothing to tell, sir... I went to bed at 9..."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, there is your answer, so who wants soup?"

In the silence the stove ticks and a log settles in the grate with a soft crunch.

A log shifts in the stove and a spray of sparks climbs the flue; for a moment the whole long room glows orange and then dims again.

### 5
The tuft of red wool lies on its paper twist beside the salt. Nobody touches it. Rain begins again on the slates, soft at first and then steadily.

**Dilys:** "Mind, red wool, like the boy's scarf. The boy goes up there to trim. Wool snags on rough iron. It proves nothing."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, red wool, you say, and half the quay has some of that, Inspector, so it could be in any scarf or stew."

Barnaby wipes his hands slowly down his apron and picks up a ladle he has no use for.

**Pip:** "My scarf is 1 of many like it, sir..."
**Jory:** "Not the pilot's. Never wears red."
**Ottoline:** "A scrap of wool, Inspector, and the tide washes worse than that up the quay every morning."

Pip tugs the scarf higher about his neck and studies the grain of the table. A thin cold draught finds its way under the door.

The wind finds a loose slate overhead and worries at it, a small dry rattle above the ceiling, and then lets it go. Outside, a halyard slaps against a mast somewhere along the quay, steady as a slow clock, and no one in the room remarks on it.

### 6
The wind leans on the shutters. Jory stares into the stove for a while before he speaks. Down on the quay a late cart rattles past the window, its lantern swinging.

**Jory:** "Half tide. Not high water."
**Jory:** "High Light sat low. A hair left."
**Jory:** "Not sure. Trusted the lights. Always do."
**Ottoline:** "The lights do not lie, Inspector. That is the rule of this tide and every tide before it."
**Ottoline:** "As for the engineer, ask me about the tide tables. They are in order and they are on the shelf."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the keeper reports to the harbour-master. The Board's men come and go. The keeper tends the lamps."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, Thursday it was, then, the night the wind got up at supper, and the pie went cold."
**Pip:** "I will fetch the ink from the desk, sir, there are 2 bottles..."

Pip sets a stoppered ink bottle beside her notebook.
**Pip:** "I heard the cutter's bell at 10..."

Ottoline reaches for the tide table on the wall and runs a finger down the column without reading it.

The clock on the wall gives the quarter hour, thin and a little slow, and the smell of damp wool and woodsmoke settles deeper into the room.

### 7
The smell of baked pastry has taken over the room. Barnaby lifts the lid of a pot, then the lid of another, and keeps his back to the table. The kettle on the hob begins its low rising note.

**Barnaby:** "Now then, not the herring pie for you, Inspector, for I have eggs, a heel of cheese and the day's bread, so I will fetch them from the scullery myself."

He lifts the kettle aside with the corner of his apron and goes through the hatch. The door swings to behind him.

**Dilys:** "Mind, eighty steps, Inspector, and the knee counts every one."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the keeper signed the log without the climb. That is the keeper's shame, and the keeper says it plain."
**Ottoline:** "A person must eat before the tide turns, Inspector."
**Jory:** "Climb is long. Wet steps."
**Pip:** "I will fetch 2 plates from the shelf, sir..."

Pip is already moving, glad of the errand, his right hand still in his pocket. Rain ticks against the glass in little handfuls.

The lamp over the table swings a hand's breadth on its chain and settles again, and the shadows on the stone floor slide with it.

### 8
Barnaby comes back through the hatch with the tray and sets it down without a word. He has been drying the same pan for some time before Wren's question lands, and he sets it down very gently on the hob.

**Barnaby:** "Now then, I was in the scullery with the pot and the pan, and I heard nothing of it, Inspector."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, I saw no boy and no oil, Inspector, for the bread kept me at the oven all evening."

He wipes his hands down his apron, twice, and does not look toward the pegs.

**Ottoline:** "The tide gives every man his own evening, Inspector, and I ask none to account for another's."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the boy comes down by the bluff path. That is the only path there is."
**Jory:** "Dark path. Steep too."
**Pip:** "I was at the pegs, sir, with 2 oilskins to hang..."

The lamp over the table swings a little on its chain. Somewhere in the roof a single drip falls into a bucket, patient and regular, and the stove ticks to keep it company.

Rain ticks against the glass in little handfuls, and a thin draught from under the door stirs the hems of the oilskins on their pegs.

### 9
At the far end of the hall the oilskins rustle on their pegs. Pip stands very still for a long moment, and then his shoulders drop.

**Pip:** "My Da used to say that... It was Thursday, at dusk, and I went up with the oil can, 80 steps..."
**Pip:** "I knocked the lamp on the bracket in the dark, just 1 knock... It tilted. I pushed it back by eye..."
**Pip:** "The brass pin came out bent... I put it in my pocket. It is here, 1 pin..."
**Pip:** "The Board put Da out for a dark lamp... I did not want the keeper put out for a crooked one. So I said nothing for 4 days..."

Down the hall the others carry on without a glance in his direction.

**Ottoline:** "The tide is turning, Inspector's business or not."
**Jory:** "Late already. Rain coming."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the stove wants peat."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, who wants more tea?"

Rain ticks against the glass in little handfuls, and a thin draught from under the door stirs the hems of the oilskins on their pegs. Somewhere in the roof a single drip falls into a bucket, patient and regular, and every head lifts a little toward it before settling.

### 10
Wren comes back alone. Pip stays down by the pegs, looking at nothing, one hand still in his pocket. The long table is quiet now, and a moth has found the lamp and circles it, ticking against the glass.

**Jory:** "Boy said nothing. Not tonight."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the keeper says no such thing. The boy goes up wet or dry."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the bracket stands where it always stands. The keeper knows nothing of it. From the window it looks steady."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, I have nothing to add about the boy, Inspector, but the tea is fresh."
**Ottoline:** "The Heron, Inspector, not the Gannet. The tide took my husband twenty years ago, and it took the Heron with him."
**Jory:** "Same boat. Father too."
**Pip:** "I am here, by the 3 pegs... I did not say a word to anyone, not 1 word..."

Ottoline's hand rests flat on the tide table for a moment, and nobody looks at her. A draught lifts the corner of the Board's crest on the table, and nobody reaches to hold it down.

Somewhere in the roof a single drip falls into a bucket, patient and regular, and every head lifts a little toward it before settling.

### 11
The paper lies open on the table, the Board's crest at the top. For a moment nobody breathes. Then Ottoline's back, so straight all evening, bends a little.

**Ottoline:** "It is so. The bracket was condemned fourteen months ago, and the tide of that winter took the repair money into the mess roof."
**Ottoline:** "I put the notice in my desk. I meant to find the sixty pounds by spring. The tide went out, Inspector, and I did not."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the keeper does not know of it. The keeper would climb those steps on her knees."
**Jory:** "Never told us. Not once."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, the Master kept it to herself, then, and well she might, with the roof and the rain and the brandy bills."
**Pip:** "I heard 1 word, and it was 14..."

Nobody looks at the harbour-master. Nobody looks away either. Somewhere in the roof the bucket takes another drip.

The wind finds a loose slate overhead and worries at it, a small dry rattle above the ceiling, and then lets it go. Outside, a halyard slaps against a mast somewhere along the quay, steady as a slow clock, and no one in the room remarks on it.

### 12
Barnaby sets down his ladle. This time he does not wipe his hands, because he keeps them very still on the edge of the table.

**Barnaby:** "Now then, nine loaves, Inspector, as I said, and the oven door stuck, and I never left the scullery before supper."

He wipes his hands down his apron after he has said it.

**Ottoline:** "The steward has told the same tale to the tide and to the Board, Inspector, and it will not change."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the keeper cannot say where the steward stands. The scullery has a hatch, and the hatch faces the table."
**Jory:** "Scullery door faces yard."
**Pip:** "I did see 1 light in the yard, sir..."

The kettle is cold now, the bread board bare, and the lamp over the table burns low and yellow, throwing five long shadows across the stone floor of the hall.

Outside, the wind finds a loose slate and worries at it, a small dry rattle above the ceiling, and then lets it go.

The clock on the wall gives the quarter hour, thin and a little slow, and the smell of damp wool and woodsmoke settles deeper into the room.

### 13
Wren buttons her coat. The stove ticks. Pip, already on his feet, takes the black umbrella from its place by the oilskins and holds it out before she can look for it.

**Pip:** "Your umbrella, Inspector... It is 1 long climb to the stair..."
**Ottoline:** "Mine. My name goes in it, Inspector. The tide does not carry a bracket for anyone else."
**Ottoline:** "The boy and the keeper stand clear of it, and the tide will hear me say so before any Board."
**Jory:** "She means it. Write it."
**Dilys:** "Mind, the harbour-master is no liar. The lamps burn at dawn, Inspector."
**Barnaby:** "Now then, sleep well, and I will have the kettle and the bread ready at half past four."
**Ottoline:** "A tide table for the girl Ife, Inspector, printed for this harbour, and the tide will not fail her."

The wind drops a little against the shutters, as though the harbour itself were letting out a breath.

The clock on the wall gives the half hour, thin and a little slow, and no one remarks on it.

Steam lifts from the cups on the table and drifts sideways in the draught, and the bench creaks as somebody shifts their weight on it. A cart rolls slowly along the quay outside, its wheels loud on the wet stones, and then the sound fades away toward the sea wall.
