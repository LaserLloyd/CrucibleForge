### 1
Thursday already feeling long is its own kind of tired. Kettle's on over here, so take the chair that doesn't wobble. A toast for your little sister is a lovely thing to be handed, even if it was handed to you like a hot pan. Delia picked you, and that says something about the two of you. And Moss sounds like a dog who has found his calling. One sock is an appetiser. Our old library cat once ate most of a bookmark ribbon and then sat on the returns desk looking like he'd earned a medal. Has the toast started to take shape in your head yet, even if nothing's on paper?

### 2
Quiet and good, which is my favourite kind. I spent the light picking the last of the cherry tomatoes before the frost gets them, and Parsnip supervised from the green watering can, which is where he sits when he wants credit for gardening. Then I sorted two boxes for the library's autumn book sale. Somebody donated forty paperbacks about submarines. Forty. I made them a little display called Deep Reading and felt very pleased with myself, though I expect nobody else will laugh. Now I'm out here with my tea and the moths. What sort of day did Thursday give you?

### 3
Four in the morning is a heroic hour to be on your feet, and it explains why you sound like a man running on crumbs by evening. Good of Gus to take Moss on Saturday; tell him to hide his socks. And thank you for telling me about the sesame. That's the kind of thing a friend ought to know, not a footnote. I'll keep it filed right next to Delia and the toast. You're carrying a lot into this weekend, but you're carrying it in a sensible order: sleep, dog, sister, speech.

### 4
Something easy that still lets you show off, then. Lemon shortbread is my vote: butter, sugar, flour, zest, an hour of your evening, and it travels in a tin without sulking. Skip the sesame-crusted crackers people love to bring to these lunches, since you'd be eating around your own contribution, and check any bought biscuits for tahini or sesame oil while you're at it. If you want it to look like a baker made it, press a fork pattern on top and dust it with a little sugar. Simple food made carefully always wins a rehearsal lunch.

### 5
That's a rotten evening. Both trays gone, a boss shouting at you on top of it, and Delia quiet just when you want her to pick up. No wonder you've had enough of today. You don't have to fix any of it tonight, or be cheerful about it for me. I'm just here on the porch. Put the trays in to soak, make yourself something warm, and let the day be bad for a while. It's allowed.

### 6
Not tonight, is my honest answer. You start at four, and a toast written at midnight by a man who has to be up before the birds will sound exactly like that. Go to bed early. Tomorrow, when your shift ends around lunchtime, eat something, take Moss round the block and talk the toast out loud to him. Write down the three things you say that make you smile. That's your speech. On Friday you read it through once, well before nine, and then you leave it alone. Does that fit around your Friday?

### 7
I'd support the claim. Dependents are people you feed, house and clothe, and Moss is plainly clothing himself in your socks. Keep the receipts: one pair, grey, tragically unmatched. If the tax office queries it, tell them he's in apprenticeship as a laundry inspector and the work is very hands-on. Parsnip would file as self-employed and charge me rent for the watering can. Between the two of them we could start a small, deeply unprofitable family business.

### 8
Bed is right, and before the shoes is wise. For Saturday: drop Moss at Gus's with his lead and something of yours that isn't a sock. Put the toast in your jacket pocket on Friday night so it can't hide. At the reception, ask the caterer which plates have sesame before you eat anything, even the nice-looking bread. And when you stand up for Delia, look at her, not the paper. She'll keep that part longest. Sleep well, early riser. Kettle's on whenever you're back.
