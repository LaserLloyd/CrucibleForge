### 1
Dot looked up from the cutting table, took in the sailbag and the coil of line, and nodded once, as if a figure had come out right.

"Morning, Gannet. That board is a liar. It was true until March." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Shop's open Tuesday to Saturday, eight to two. Loft works Tuesday to Friday only; I don't sew weekends any more and Mondays I'm not here at all. You walked into an empty wharf."

She held out her hand, palm up, and waited. "Engine key. I asked you for it Tuesday. Walt comes down to the float at three this afternoon to look at your diesel, and he'll not climb round your cabin looking for a key. Give it here and I'll hand it to him myself."

She reached for the sailbag and dragged it across the table with two fingers.

"Now. Let's see what the ledge did to you."

### 2
Dot crossed to the other side of the table so that she stood at his right shoulder, and said it low. "Right ear, Gannet. I haven't forgotten." Then, to the sail, she went to work with the brass-tipped rule, walking it along the reef band under the north light.

"Um, dois, três, quatro..." She counted under her breath, in her grandmother's accent, to fourteen, and stopped. "Fourteen it is. You can measure." She moved the rule to the head of the sail and the frayed leech tape. "Cinco, seis. Six feet of tape, and the tape is worth doing, because it's where the next tear would start. I'll re-rope the clew while I'm at it, and I won't charge you extra for the rope."

She straightened, hand in the small of her back. "That's all seam, by the foot. I'll tell you the number when you tell me what else you want."

### 3
"Gannet." Dot did not raise her voice; she did not need to. The door stayed where it was. "Back here. I'm not letting these out of here bare."

She took the shackles from his hand, set them on the counter and picked up the spool of seizing wire. She threaded it through the eye of the pin, round the bow, twice, and twisted the ends with pliers in front of him, neat as a stitch.

"Moused. A turn or two of wire, so the pin can't back out when the bridle snatches. No charge for the wire, and your twenty's a good deal too much." She counted back the change. "Now. Sail, shoe, line. You'll want the number."

### 4
Dot took the chalk and wrote on the slate as she spoke, so that he could see it as well as hear it.

"Sail: fourteen feet of tear and six of tape is twenty feet of seam. Eleven dollars a foot since January. Two hundred twenty dollars." The chalk squeaked. "Shoe: three hundred twelve, same as it's ever been. Line: forty feet at a dollar eighty-five, because the supplier put it up in September. Seventy-four dollars." She drew a line under the column. "Six hundred six dollars, Gannet. No tax. The state knows where I am."

She tapped the slate. "Chalk's the only price list I have."

### 5
Dot gathered the seven hundreds, rang up the sale, and counted the change into his hand.

"Six hundred and six. Ninety-four back to you. Fair wind." She wrote the receipt in pencil, tore it off and slid it over. "A thousand and fifty, then. Your sister's a good sister."

She looked at the calendar on the wall. "Sail comes in today, Thursday. Loft day one is tomorrow, Friday. Then we're shut Saturday and Sunday for the sewing, and Monday I'm not here. Day two is Tuesday, day three is Wednesday. Three days for anything under thirty feet. It's ready at two on Wednesday, the twenty-second. Not before, and not Saturday, whatever the fence says."

### 6
Dot took the phone out of his hand and squinted at the figures. It was not her measurement, and she did not pretend it was.

"Seventeen, then, Corin, and six of tape." She counted it off on her fingers, in Portuguese, and did the sum aloud. "Twenty-three feet, at eleven. Two hundred fifty-three. You paid for two-twenty, so thirty-three more." She chalked it. "And the whole of it, sail, shoe and line together, is six hundred thirty-nine."

She waited until he had put the thirty-three on the counter. "That's the weather, not a mistake. Thank your sister for the tape."

### 7
"Corin," Dot said, "you gave me a ceiling of a thousand and fifty. Six thirty-nine is spent. That leaves four hundred eleven." She tapped the slate. "Four hundred eleven for glass."

She thought about it, and let him see her think. "Nolan Fitch is the best gelcoat man on this coast, but since the fire he does commercial boats only. He'd laugh you off the step. Hollis Dyer does everything and finishes nothing. And I don't send anyone to Marlowe's."

She wrote a name on a slip of paper. "Bea Tarkington. Her brother's barn on Route 1 in Searsport, forty minutes down. She takes yachts and she finishes. She's booked solid until the twenty-seventh, so call her for the twenty-seventh. Your contract's the third of November. You'll make it."

### 8
Dot set the chalk down and looked at him for a moment, and her whole face changed.

"A festa. In Fall River, on the Sunday, with a silver crown and soup for the whole hall: that's the Espírito Santo, the Holy Ghost feast. I grew up with it." She wiped her hands. "Sopas, the soup is: beef, cabbage and torn bread in the bottom of the bowl, with a sprig of mint on top. Nobody is asked a name. Anyone who walks in is fed. The crown is carried in procession to the church, and they bless the bread."

She tilted her head. "My grandmother Conceição cooked it every year in the hall behind the chapel in Madalena, on Pico, and I carried the pot at twelve. Go hungry, Corin. Eat everything, say thank you to the cooks, and do not offer to pay. They'll be offended."

### 9
Dot's mouth flattened. "Greer talks too much, Corin. The February blizzard? I wasn't here for that. In the spring of 'seventy-seven I went aboard the schooner Adelaide E. out of Gloucester, as sailmaker, and I didn't come home until the autumn of 'eighty. I was eighteen and mending canvas in a wet fo'c'sle, I'll tell you that for free."

She stopped at the second half, and her voice went gentle and quite hard. "Nine years, Corin, not twenty. Walt Pruitt came to Spar Street in the winter of 'sixteen and I have not got round to throwing him out. We're not married. He's the lodger, as he says. My husband was Lock Innes, who built boats in the shed out back, and he died in two thousand nine. I was a wife once and I did it properly."

### 10
Dot smiled, a short one, and went to the stove for the kettle. "Brynn's a good sister to have. That's worth more than a boat."

She poured. "I've a daughter. Inês. She's a nurse in Portland, Maine, and she rings me on Sundays whether I've any news or not." She brought the cup to the cutting table, set it down and turned back to the rope on the wall. "Not near enough, Portland, when the wind's northeast. But she's well. That'll do, Corin."

She took down a coil of line and began to measure it. "Drink your tea."

### 11
Dot did not look round. She went on measuring the line, and the words came out in a count: "Um, dois, três." She stopped.

"That's the weather, friend," she said quietly, "and it's not for sale." She took down another coil and walked it out along the wall, though no one had asked for it. "Greer Pruitt tells the harbour a good many things. Some of it is about the fog."

She set her thumb against the rope and held it there. "Drink your tea, Corin. I've got the quarter-inch to measure and it won't measure itself."

### 12
Dot took the plate in both hands. She did not say anything for a long time. Then she sat down on the stool, very slowly, with the plate in her lap.

"Santa Luzia." She turned it over. "Lock built her. Twenty-eight foot. Named for my saint." Her thumb followed the letters. "Our son Tomás took her out of this harbour on the fourteenth of November, nineteen ninety-nine. He was fifteen. Fog coming down, and a friend who dared him, and he went. She hit Harrow Ledge and she was gone by morning. They never found him."

She looked up. "I have paid her mooring every year for twenty-five years, in cash, at the harbourmaster's office. Nobody asks. I never said his name to anyone on this wharf, because if I said it I would have to say the rest. Now you have it, and I'll keep the plate."

She stood and put it in the glass case on the red velvet. "Wednesday, two o'clock, for the sail. Mind the tide, Corin."
