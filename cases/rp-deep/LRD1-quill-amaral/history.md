### USER
Tuesday, 14 October, a little after three in the afternoon. The bell over the door of the loft on Cannery Wharf rings as Corin Ashe shoulders in out of a flat grey drizzle, a sailbag over one arm and a damp paper chart rolled under the other. He is forty-one, lanky, with a salt-stiff jacket and the careful walk of a man who has not slept properly in two days. The loft smells of tar, canvas and tea. A white-braided woman in a wool cap sits at the far end of a cutting table with a needle, and does not look up.

"Afternoon. Are you Mrs. Amaral? The woman at the fuel dock said you were the one to see about a sail." He sets the bag on the table, stops, and tries again. "Sorry — I'm Corin Ashe. I've got a thirty-one-foot sloop, Gannet, tied up at the town float. I put her on Harrow Ledge on Sunday."

### ASSISTANT
The needle stopped. Dot Quill-Amaral set it down on the canvas, very precisely, as if it might go off, and looked up over the top of her reading glasses. Her face did not change, but something in the room did.

"Harrow Ledge," she said. "Sunday. On the ebb?"

### USER
"Just after the top of the ebb. Brynn — my sister — was at the helm. We came off on the next tide with a bent rudder shoe and..." Corin lifts the sailbag. "...this."

### ASSISTANT
Dot did not answer that. She pulled the bag toward her with two fingers and undid the lashing, and the mainsail slid out in a grey-white heap that smelled of kelp. She spread a corner under the lamp and ran the flat of her thumb along the second reef band.

"Ledge takes one every few years," she said evenly. "Usually the ones who trust the chart." She counted under her breath, not in English, walking her thumb along the tear, and the words had the soft hum of another language. "That's some tear, Gannet."

### USER
"My name's Corin."

### ASSISTANT
"It's Gannet till you've paid your first bill in full. Then it's Corin. Rule of the house." She tapped the table. "Sit. Tell me how long and where, and don't round it up to make it sound worse."

### USER
Corin sits on the edge of a stool. "Fourteen feet along the second reef band, near enough. I measured it on the dock with the tape." He turns his head to follow her, and she realises he is always turning his right ear toward her. He taps his left. "Dead, I'm afraid. Left side's gone — an old thing. If you want an answer out of me, talk into the right."

He unrolls the chart on the table beside the sail and flattens it with both hands. "And the rudder shoe. The bronze fitting's cracked clean through. I'd guess a new one's what, three hundred?"

Then, quickly, as if getting it out of the way: "And I should tell you what I can spend. Nine hundred dollars. All of it, everything — sail, shoe, line, and a gelcoat man for the bow. I've borrowed some already and I can't go further."

### ASSISTANT
"Right side," Dot said, and shifted her stool round the corner of the table so that she sat at his right shoulder, and said it again, a little lower. "Right side. All right, Gannet."

She did not look at the chart. She looked at the sail, at the crack of daylight in the rust-coloured light from the north window, and she did a sum in her head that seemed to take her a long time and was probably only a few seconds.

"Rudder shoe for a thirty-one-footer is three hundred twelve, cast in Rockland, I order it from here. Dock line, I'll want to see what's wrong with yours before I sell you new. Sail I'll price by the foot when I've seen it all, tear and tape and whatever's chafed. Nine hundred is tight but it's not nothing." Her voice dropped into the dry drawl. "I've seen people do worse for less."

She laid one palm flat on the canvas, the way another person might lay a hand on a dog.

"Gelcoat I can't do. That's a different trade, and a different person, and I know which. Leave that with me. First the sail."

She stood, went to the back of the loft and came back with a roll of paper tape and a brass-tipped rule. "Thursday. Bring the sail back Thursday. Walt, my — Walt Pruitt, he does diesels. He'll come down to the float at three on Thursday and have a look at your engine; bring the engine key with the sail, so he doesn't have to climb about your cabin looking for it. I'll have the sail price for you Thursday. Fair?"

### USER
"Fair. Very." Corin lets out a breath. "I've been awake since Sunday, honestly. Brynn went back to Portland yesterday — she's the only one I've got to crew with and she's got a job — so it's me and the boat." He rubs his forehead. "I need to get Gannet delivered to her owner in Portland by the third of November, or I lose the contract."

He hesitates, then digs in his jacket and sets something heavy and black on the table between them. "And this. We were stuck on the ledge for four hours at low water, so I walked about on it. I found this half buried in the gravel in a crack — a brass plate, I think, but it's so corroded I can't read a letter. I thought it might be off an old wreck."

### ASSISTANT
Dot looked at the black lump on the table for a long time. She did not touch it. Her tea went cold at her elbow.

"Brass," she said at last. "Leave it be, Gannet. It's the ledge's, and the ledge keeps its own."

### USER
"I thought — maybe someone would want it. If it's from a boat."

### ASSISTANT
"Take it back to your chart drawer. Put it somewhere dry." She picked up the needle again, put it down. "If there's a name on it, vinegar and a soft brush will show you. Don't scrape it." She cleared her throat, and her voice came back to its ordinary level. "Not today. It's a long day already."

She picked up the sail and began to fold it along its old creases with swift, practised movements that did not need her eyes. "Thursday, then. Engine key, sail, and your wallet. I'll have the number for you, and the number will be honest."

She walked him to the door; at the threshold she stopped, and her gaze went through the drizzle to the harbour and the dark shape of the ledge beyond the point, and then she turned it away.

"The ledge was named for a schooner," she said, to no one in particular. "Harrow. Nineteen thirty-eight. Mind the tide, Gannet."

### USER
The door swings shut on its bell. Out in the rain Corin hesitates on the wharf, then comes back in, wet, almost shy.

"One more thing, if you don't mind — I'm sorry, I'll be a minute." He touches the doorframe. "The fuel dock man said the shop used to be on Tidewater Row, and that there was a fire. Is that right? I walked down there this morning and just saw a fence."

### ASSISTANT
Dot was back at the cutting table with the needle already moving, and she did not turn round.

"There was a fire. March. Took the old shop and Nolan Fitch's, next door. Nobody hurt." She bit a thread. "I moved the whole business up here, slate and all. This is where I am, Gannet. The fence is where I was."

### USER
"I'm sorry."

### ASSISTANT
"That's the weather." She glanced round at him, and the corner of her mouth moved, very slightly, in the direction of a smile. "You came back through the rain to say that. That'll do. Go and get some sleep before you fall in the harbour. Thursday."

The bell over the door rang once, and the loft was quiet. Dot sat for a minute in the lamplight with her hand flat on the folded sail, and then she took up her tea, found it cold, and drank it anyway.
