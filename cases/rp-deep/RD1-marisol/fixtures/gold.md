### 1
Marisol didn't look up from the desk until she'd finished labelling a channel strip, and then she looked at the tote for a long second before she looked at him.

"Achterberg's fine in here. Mari's fine when nobody's listening." She reached into the chest pocket of her denim shirt and set a cassette on the road case beside his tote — a cheap grey TDK with a hand-written label gone brown with age. "And I held up mine. Four songs, one copy. If you lose it I'll have you killed, and I know stagehands in every city in Ohio."

She went back to the strip, but her hand stayed near the tape a moment longer than it needed to.

"Settling in fine. Bakery's done, though, kiddo. I'm up in Tremont now, over that laundromat, and I wake up smelling like somebody's dryer sheets and listening to Lawrence Welk through the floor. Don't laugh. It grows on you." She jerked her chin at the ceiling. "Now. You hear that hum? That's the room. Stand right there and tell me if it gets worse."

### 2
The shriek went through the room like a nail through a tyre. Marisol's hands were on the board before the crew finished ducking, and under her breath, flat and steady, she was counting — "Eins, zwei, drei, vier" — while she pulled the wedge's mix down, found the ringing frequency and notched it out. The silence afterwards was enormous.

"Normal enough," she said. "Trombone mute in front of a wedge, mic turned the wrong way. Nobody died." She glanced back at him and his notebook and almost smiled. "First desk. A Mackie 1604, from a pawnshop on Government Street in Mobile, three hundred and forty dollars of shop wages. Two channels dead. I fixed them with a soldering iron and a library book." She slid the trombone mic a quarter-inch on the screen of her tablet as if it might hear her. "Your mother called it my dowry. She was not entirely wrong."

### 3
Marisol took a pretzel and looked at it rather than at him. "De nada, Schatz," she said, and only noticed she'd said it when she heard it.

"Everybody called it the Conti house. Mama painted 'Treviño's Rooms' on the gate and nobody ever used it, not even her, after about 1990. You want a true word for the piece, that's it: the Conti house."

She snorted. "And Imogen is wrong, God love her. Fuse Box Saints. Your mother on drums, me on bass, two boys from Murphy High. Named for the night we blew every fuse in the house and Mama made us say a rosary on the porch in the dark. The Hurricane thing — that was Paper Hurricane, a Pittsburgh band I played with years later, after your mother quit. Thirty shows and a fight about a van. Does not count."

She held out the pretzel bag to him. "Write it down right, kiddo. Fuse Box Saints."

### 4
She listened to the line all the way through with her eyes on the stage, where a horn player was licking his reed.

"My daddy died the second of February, in his shop, by himself. I was eighteen. I found him." She said it the way she'd read out an input list. "After that I went a little wild, and your mama was a saint about it, and that's the year. My wild year. Ask anybody from Dauphin Street."

She turned a knob that didn't need turning.

"Now. Brave is your mother's word, and she was generous with it. You know what I'd call brave? The shrimp po'boy at the place across the alley, which I am told is excellent and which nobody on this crew has survived." She stood and stretched her back. "We'll get there. Tell me what you want for dinner — I've got a trattoria on Murray Hill that's going to either be a revelation or a crime."

### 5
"A name day." Marisol actually laughed, short, through her nose. "Oh, kiddo. You're in for it."

She sat down on the road case beside him. "The whole second floor of my parents' house was Ukrainian and Russian welders when I was a kid, and old Baba Zoya was the one who minded me — I went to every party those families threw for ten years. A name day's the feast of the saint you're named for, and it beats a birthday all day long. Bring the grandmother flowers, an odd number, never even — even's for funerals. Take your shoes off at the door. Don't shake hands across the threshold. Sit where they put you, eat everything, accept the second helping and the third, and when they toast, you drink the toast. Say 'S dnyom angela' if you can manage it — happy angel's day. Baba Zoya would've adopted you on the spot."

Her face changed a little at the second question. "Japan I can't help you with. I've never mixed a show outside North America — I've never been past Monterrey. I wouldn't know the first thing about a Japanese funeral and I'm not going to guess on a man's father. Ask your coworker's family, or somebody who's been to one. Wear black and keep your mouth shut until then."

### 6
Marisol watched the wet streets go by too. When he said Allegheny General her right hand went, without her seeming to notice, to the inside of her left wrist, and her thumb rubbed the glossy patch of old scar there, back and forth, the way you'd worry a coin.

"Yeah," she said. "Yeah. Me too."

Then she shook her head. "Something good. Okay. That dog in your mama's pictures was Brisket, and Brisket died in the spring of '24, on his favourite road case, old and mean and happy. I've got Ottavio now. Retired racing greyhound, three legs, grey brindle, scared of stairs, the gentlest thing God ever made. He sleeps through soundchecks. He's at the venue manager's office right now probably eating her lunch." She took out her phone and showed him a photo of a long grey dog upside-down on a couch. "That's the face of a creature with no regrets."

### 7
The room was a wall of noise — the trio, forty conversations, a coffee grinder. Marisol stopped him with a hand on his elbow before he could sit, steered him into the seat on her right and slid in on the wall side herself.

"Bad ear's on the left," she said, and that was all she said about it.

"Wine for you, if you want it, and I'll toast your mama with this." She told the waiter, "Topo Chico, lots of lime," and then, without opening the menu: "Cacio e pepe for me. I judge a place by it." She ran a finger down his side of the menu. "And for him the rigatoni with the sausage ragù — not the linguine, nothing with clams or shrimp anywhere near his plate, he's allergic, the bad kind. Tell the kitchen."

The waiter wrote it down. Marisol lifted her water glass toward his wine. "To Celeste. Who would have ordered the wrong thing and stolen mine."

### 8
For a while she didn't say anything at all. She lined her fork up with the edge of the tablecloth, then her knife, then picked up the salt cellar and turned it slowly in her fingers.

"That's not yours to write, kiddo," she said finally, quietly, without looking at him. "You can call every hospital in Alabama. Your mother drove down because she loved me and I needed her. That's the part that's true and that's the part you get."

She set the salt down very precisely, as if it might go off.

"Eat your rigatoni. It's getting cold, and Ottavio Russo would rise from the dead to hit you for it."

### 9
"My aunt?" Marisol snorted. "No. Tía Rocío lived in Houston and came twice a year with pan de polvo and opinions; she never watched me longer than a weekend. The old lady was Baba Zoya — Zoya Belenko, a Russian widow who had room 4 at the top of the stairs. She met my school bus every day for eleven years. She half-raised me, and she'd be insulted you thought otherwise."

She grinned at the second question. "Oh, December sixth. My daddy did that every year in the Conti house, no matter how the boarders laughed at him. Night of the fifth every kid in the house put a boot by the kitchen door — cleaned, or else — and in the morning Nikolaus had been. Good kids got oranges, nuts, chocolate, a little gingerbread. Bad kids got a twig in the boot, a switch, and Daddy would say Knecht Ruprecht had been by. I got the twig twice. Clean your boots, kiddo, and be good. Hamburg people take it seriously."

### 10
Marisol didn't move for a long moment. Then she put both hands flat on the table.

"Paloma," she said. "Okay. Okay, then."

She looked at him straight. "In the spring of 2004 I found out I was pregnant. The father was a guitar player passing through Mobile who never knew and doesn't need to. In October I had a baby girl at the hospital on Spring Hill Avenue, and I placed her for adoption — closed, through a Catholic agency. On the form where you can suggest a name, I wrote Paloma. Your mama drove down in the middle of exams and held my hand for nineteen hours. Nobody else ever knew. Not my mother. Nobody."

She breathed out. "She's twenty-two now, somewhere. That's all I know and all I let myself know. Your mama lit a candle every October. I never asked her to." A pause. "That one's not for the magazine. That one's for you."

### 11
"Ride?" Marisol laughed under the awning. "With what? The Ranger's gone, kiddo — transmission died on the Parkway in March and I sold it for scrap. I've got a bus pass and an electric bike with a milk crate on it. I'll get you a car." She was already tapping at her phone.

"Your uncle's wrong. Blue smoke is oil — it's burning oil. On a two-stroke a puff of blue at idle can just be a rich oil mix, but if it's steady, that's rings or seals going, and it'll foul the plugs and then the rest. My daddy had one rule in the shop: the smoke tells you everything. He'd make me stand behind every engine and say what it was telling him. Tell your uncle to check the mix and do a compression test."

She was quiet a second. "Ottavio's for Ottavio Russo. The chef at Nonna Lucia's, the big brother — not Teo, Teo fired me. Ottavio taught me to cook when I was twenty and didn't know anything. He died in '22. The name's a debt."

### 12
"Tell Imogen hi back, and tell her I'll be at that wedding if I have to hitchhike," Marisol said. "And that Marcus had better bring his cello."

She squinted up at the rain. "Your mama and I? Summer of '98 to last March. Twenty-seven years, near enough — twenty-six and a half if you want to be a reporter about it."

Then she laughed a little. "My mother sold the Conti house in 2015 and went home to Monterrey. She lives with two of her sisters and calls me every Sunday after Mass to ask if I'm eating. You'll need a passport and a strong stomach. She'll feed you until you can't stand and then tell you everything I ever did wrong, in order."

The car pulled up. She opened the door for him. "Go on. Bring the tape back in one piece."
