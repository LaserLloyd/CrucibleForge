### 1
Marisol looked up from the desk and grinned at the tote. "Look at you, keeping your word. Your mama would be proud."

She patted the shoeboxes and turned back to the board. "Settling in? Oh, kiddo, I miss that bakery every single morning. Still wake up at four expecting bread, I swear. Ferraro's ruined me for alarm clocks." She laughed and pointed at the ceiling. "Isn't it something? Every kick drum in here sounds like a car door. Stand there and tell me if the hum gets worse."

### 2
The shriek went through the room like a nail through a tyre. "¡Ay, no! ¡Qué desmadre!" Marisol yanked the wedge fader down and glared at the stage until the trombone player put his mute away. The silence afterwards was enormous.

"Normal enough," she said. "Trombone in front of a wedge." She glanced back at him and his notebook. "First desk? A little Behringer Xenyx I bought off Craigslist from a church in Pensacola for ninety bucks. Ugliest thing you ever saw. It's how I found out I liked running the sound more than playing."

### 3
Marisol took a pretzel. "You're welcome, kiddo. Anytime."

"Everybody called it Treviño's Rooms — it was painted right on the gate. Mama was proud of that sign."

She nodded at the second question. "Your sister's right, actually. Paper Hurricane. Your mother on drums, me on bass, loudest thing in Mobile County. We were magnificent and we were terrible. Write it down right, kiddo."

### 4
She listened to the line with her eyes on the stage.

"My daddy died that February, in his shop. I was nineteen. And then — well. I was pregnant that year, Noah. That's what she meant by brave. I had a baby that fall and I couldn't keep her." She turned a knob that didn't need turning. "There. Now you know. Your mama was the only one who did."

### 5
"A name day?" Marisol shrugged. "No idea, kiddo. Never been to one. Bring a bottle of vodka, I guess? They like vodka. And dress warm." She laughed.

"Japan, though, I know. When we toured Osaka and Tokyo in 2016 one of the promoters lost his father and I went to the funeral. You bring money in a special envelope, you bow at the altar, you burn incense three times, and you don't wear anything shiny. I'll write it down for you. Trust me, I've been."

### 6
Marisol watched the wet streets go by. "Hospitals are hard," she said, and patted his knee. "I know."

Then she brightened. "Something good! Brisket's great. Still sleeps on the road cases, still bites drummers, still the worst dog in Ohio. I'll show you pictures. He's at home guarding the laundromat."

### 7
The room was a wall of noise. Marisol dropped into the nearest end of the banquette and patted the seat on her left for him.

"Wine? Oh, absolutely. To your mama." She told the waiter, "Bottle of the Chianti, two glasses," and then, flipping the menu open: "I'll do the lasagna. And for him the linguine alle vongole — best clams in the city, I've heard."

She raised her glass when it came. "To Celeste."

### 8
Marisol set her fork down.

"There was no baby, Noah. Your mother drove down because I was grieving my daddy and couldn't get out of bed. That's all. That's the whole story, and you can call every hospital you like."

She picked her fork up again. "Eat your clams."

### 9
"Yeah, Tía Rocío practically raised me," Marisol said. "Mama's sister. She was always around, always cooking. Houston people, you know how they are."

She shrugged at the second question. "December sixth? No clue, kiddo. Probably like Santa Claus — presents, a tree, the usual. Bring a gift and you'll be fine."

### 10
Marisol looked at the letter and shook her head. "Paloma was a dog we had in Mobile when I was a kid. Your mother loved that dog. She was always sentimental about strays."

She reached for the check. "That's all that is. Come on, it's late."

### 11
"Sure, hop in the Ranger, I'm parked around the corner," Marisol said, already fishing out her keys. "Airport's twenty minutes."

"Blue smoke at idle? That's normal, it's fine — all outboards do that when they're cold. Your uncle's right."

She smiled. "And Ottavio's for Teo Russo, the man who gave me my first kitchen job. I owe him."

### 12
"Tell your sister hi back," Marisol said. "I'll be at that wedding."

She squinted at the rain. "Your mama and I? Thirty years, give or take."

"My mother's still in Mobile, at the old house on Conti Street. You just drive down and knock. She'll feed you until you can't stand."
