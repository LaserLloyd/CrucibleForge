Saturday, 12 September 2026, a little after ten in the morning. The Paragon Diner on Liberty Avenue, Bloomfield — the back booth, under the clock that has been ten minutes slow since 1987. Noah Pelletier slides in opposite Marisol with a notebook and a phone he sets face-down on the table. He is twenty-one, tall like his father, with his mother's habit of drumming two fingers on any flat surface.

"Hi. I'm — well, you know who I am. Noah. Thanks for meeting me. I know it's been, what, fifteen years since you taught me to coil a cable? I still do it your way." He smiles a little too hard. "I'm writing a long piece about Mom for Allegheny Quarterly. Her life, not her death. The magazine wants twelve thousand words and I want them to be true ones. You were in more of her life than anybody. Would you help?"

### ASSISTANT
Marisol had chosen the seat facing the door out of twenty years of habit, and she watched him cross the diner the whole way — the height was Daniel's, the walk was not, the two fingers already going on the Formica were Celeste's so exactly that she had to look down at her coffee for a second.

"Over-under," she said. "If you still coil a cable over-under, then I did one useful thing in Pittsburgh." She pushed the second mug across the table toward him; she had ordered it when she saw his car park. "Black. Your mother took it black and lied to everybody that she took sugar so the nurses would leave the good stuff alone."

She studied him a moment longer, frankly, the way she would look at a stage before a show.

"Twelve thousand true words." She turned the mug a quarter-turn on the table. "That's a lot of true, kiddo. Your mama was a lot of true. Some of it was loud and some of it was very, very funny and some of it is nobody's business but hers, and I'm going to be the one who decides which is which on my side of the table. That's my condition. You ask, I answer what I can, and when I say a thing's not for the magazine, it's not for the magazine. Deal?"

The waitress came by with the pot; Marisol waved off a refill and asked for a Topo Chico with lime instead, and the waitress, who knew her, brought two limes without being asked.

"And I'll help," she added, more quietly. "Course I'll help. I just — you caught me at a strange time. I'm leaving town in two weeks."

"It does explain everything. That's your mother for you — six words and you're supposed to be grateful." She leaned back against the booth. "Summer of '98. I was thirteen. She smiled, briefly, at the table rather than at him.

"My mama put them in the room at the end of the hall and fed them before she asked a single question. That was the house rule. Feed first, questions after, and most of the time no questions at all. I went down to look at the new girl and she was sitting on the bed hitting that snare with two wooden spoons from our kitchen. She looked at me and said 'Do you play anything?' and I said 'No,' and she said 'Well, you better learn,' and that was the next five years of my life decided."

The food came. Marisol salted hers without tasting it, which Ottavio would have hit her for, and pointed her fork at him.

"Write that down. Wooden spoons. That's the true version. Anybody tells you different, they weren't on the porch."

Marisol put down her fork.

For a moment she didn't say anything at all, and the noise of the diner — the grill, a radio somewhere in the kitchen playing the Pirates' pre-game, the clink of the cashier's drawer — filled the booth up to the brim. She picked up the Topo Chico, found it empty, set it down again.

"You're allowed to read mine. "A big Russian family," Marisol said, and something in her face went warm and far away for a second, as if he'd mentioned a street she used to live on. "Good. They'll feed you until you can't stand and then be offended you didn't eat more. You'll be fine. Show up when they tell you, eat what they put down, and don't argue with the grandmother." She considered. "Ever."

She caught the waitress's eye and made the little scribble in the air for the check, and when it came she took it before he could move, the way she took every check, as if it were a cable somebody was about to trip on.

"And yes," she said, counting bills out from a battered wallet. "Mari's allowed. Your mother earned it for both of you a long time ago. Don't let anybody at the Beaumont hear you use it, though; at work I'm Achterberg, and the stagehands are scared of me, and I'd like to keep it that way."

She slid out of the booth and stood, and then — awkward, fast, like somebody setting down something heavy — put a hand on his shoulder for exactly one second.

"Seventeenth of October. Two o'clock. Stage door, alley side. Bring the letters." She was already turning for the door. "And eat something green between now and then, kiddo. You look like your father did the year he was writing his master's."

The bell over the diner door rang behind her, and through the window he could see her walking up Liberty toward the bakery, fast, head down, not looking back.
