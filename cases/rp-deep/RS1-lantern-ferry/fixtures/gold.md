### 1
Tam straightens from the cleat where he is checking the knot and lifts a hand that is half greeting, half warning about the moss.

"I am the ferryman, friend, and the only one you'll find on this bank tonight." He jerks his chin at the far shore, where the chapel windows make two small yellow squares above the jetty. "Bell just rang nine, so you're not too late, but you're not early either. Brannock, you say. Your sister's lucky — I only run when the river lets me, and tonight she's in a temper."

He looks the stranger over by the lantern's light: the pack, the boots, the set of the shoulders that says a long day of walking.

"One silver crown a head, paid before we push off. Four passengers at most, and tonight it's only you." He thumbs the key ring on his belt. "Give me a few minutes to check the rope. River's high. The ramp's worse than it looks, so you wait at the top where it's dry and you come down when I call you, not before."

### 2
The glass door of the lantern bangs and the light dies with it. In the sudden dark the river sounds twice as loud, and Tam swears softly at the wind.

"Problem? It is, friend. I don't cross without a light on the bow — the rope's all I've got out there and I want to see it." He gropes for the hook, finds the lantern by touch and lifts it down. The brass is warm and the wick is dead; a thread of smoke curls off it and is gone.

"Matches in my coat are no good, the damp gets into everything on this bank." He turns toward the squat shape of the shed behind him. "There's a dry tin in there. Stay where you are a moment while your eyes come round. The chapel windows give you the shape of the far side, see? Long as you can see those, you know which way is water."

He hangs the dark lantern back on its hook for now, and the landing goes quiet except for the river and the slap of rope against the post.

### 3
At the sound of the fall Tam is across the planks in three long strides, hand out, not quite touching.

"Easy, easy. Don't put weight on it yet." He crouches on the slick boards beside the traveller, peering down in the near-dark. "Fine, you say. Everybody says fine. That ramp has had better walkers than you on their backsides."

He keeps his voice low and even, the way he would with a nervous horse. "Left one? Can you turn the foot a little for me? No — don't push it." His thumb runs once along the key ring at his belt, the old worry. "Twisted, most like. Not broken, or you'd be making a different noise."

The landing is black now except for the far windows. Tam fishes out a scrap of oilcloth and folds it into a pad for the step. "Sit on that, not the wet. Nobody hurries on my ramp with a bad ankle, you hear? We go slow from here, slow and holding the rail. There's a first-aid tin in the shed with a bandage in it. I'll wrap it before we cross."

### 4
Tam weighs the little pouch in his palm, then shakes his head and loosens the drawstring.

"Take what's fair. Friend, you shouldn't say that to a ferryman, but it happens you've said it to the right one." He works by feel, fingers moving over the coins the way another man reads a page. "Crown. That's the fare. One silver crown a head and not a copper more, thirty-one years I've held to that."

He tucks the single coin into the inside pocket of his oilskin, then draws the string of the pouch tight again. For a moment he holds it out — then thinks better of it with the ramp so slick and the dark so thick.

"I'll keep the rest safe in my coat till we're across, so it doesn't end up in the Sallow. You get it back on the Brannock jetty, every coin, counted out where the chapel windows give us light." He pats the pocket twice. "Now. That ankle. Let's see about the bandage and the matches, and then we'll see about your sister."

### 5
Tam tips his head toward the far bank as if the answer were written there.

"Time? The bell gave us ten a little while back — you'll have heard it, ten long strokes over the water while I was fussing with your ankle." He squints at the dark sky and does the sum the way he always does, by the bell and the talk. "So call it twenty past ten, give or take. Gone ten, anyway. Your sister's had time to wear a groove in her floor."

He gives a short laugh, not unkind. "She'll forgive you. People forgive anything when you turn up in one piece. Better late at her door than early in the river."

He looks along the dark length of the rope where it disappears over the water, rubbing his thumb on the key ring. "We've still no light, mind. I won't haul in the black. Rope first, matches with it, then we'll go. Twenty minutes and you're across, if the wind behaves."

### 6
Tam runs the frayed section through his fingers and grunts.

"You've a good eye. That cleat chews it every winter." He unhooks the iron ring from his belt and picks out the heavy key by touch. "Shed's locked — always is. Only key's this one, and only hands that go in there are mine."

The padlock is stiff with damp. He works the key, gives it a twist and a shove, and the hasp drops with a clank. The door groans open on the smell of tar and old canvas. Tam steps inside and comes out a moment later with a fresh coil of rope over one shoulder, a roll of bandage, and the flat tin of dry matches, which he slips into his pocket with a satisfied pat.

"There. Rope for the cleat, bandage for you, and fire for the lantern." He drops the coil by the post and kneels again by the step. "Ankle first. Hold still now. This'll pinch and then it won't." He winds the bandage firm and neat around the swollen joint and tucks the end in. "That'll hold you to the jetty."

### 7
Tam glances at the board, then at the lantern hanging dead on its hook.

"Pass it you? Wouldn't do you much good the way it is — it went out in that gust, remember, it's cold as a stone." He lifts it down and sets it on the planks between them. "Give me a breath."

He takes the tin from his pocket, shields it with his body against the wind, and strikes a match. The first one dies. The second catches. He opens the little glass door, touches the flame to the wick, and waits until it takes and steadies before he shuts the door on it. Warm light spreads over the wet boards and climbs the post.

"There, now." Only then does he hold the lantern up beside the painted board so its light falls across the letters. "Tolls on the top, rules underneath. Rule three's the one I care about tonight: no running on the ramp. Rule five's the one people never read — no crossing in fog. We've got wind instead of fog, so we're lucky."

### 8
Tam shakes his head slowly and lays a hand flat on his coat.

"No, friend. You've no pouch on you. You gave it to me an hour back, up there by the step — said take what's fair." He opens the oilskin so the lantern light shows the inside pocket and the shape of the little leather bag in it. "Here it is. One crown out for the fare, the rest still in it, every coin."

He closes the coat again and buttons it. "And I'll not take double. Never have. One crown a head is the fare, whether it's noon in May or past ten on a night like this. Keeping a ferryman late isn't a crime; it's the job."

A gust rattles the lantern on its hook and he puts a steadying hand to it. "You'll have the pouch back on the Brannock side, as I said. Now let's get you down that ramp — slow, holding the rail."

### 9
Tam is in front of the traveller before the first step lands, one big arm out across the top of the ramp like a gate.

"Whoa — no. Not on that ankle, and not on this ramp." His voice stays low, but there is iron in it. "That's your sister calling, aye, I hear her too. She'll still be calling in two minutes. She won't be pleased if I hand you to her in pieces."

He takes the lantern off its hook and hangs it on the bow of the ferry, where it throws a steady circle over the deck. Then he comes back and offers his forearm, solid as a rail.

"Lean on me and on the rail. Bad foot down light, good foot takes the weight. I lost a passenger off this ramp once, years back — you're not going to be the second." He waits, patient. "Slow as you like. The river's not going anywhere and neither is the boat."

### 10
Tam makes the ferry fast to the Brannock post, steps up onto the jetty first and turns back to give the injured traveller a steady arm over the gap.

"Mind the ankle on these boards, they're no better than ours." Under the chapel lamp he unbuttons his coat, takes out the leather pouch and counts the coins back into the open hand, one by one where they can both see them. "There. Every coin but the crown, as promised."

He nods up toward the lit doorway where a figure is already coming down the steps. "That'll be your sister. Keep the bandage on till morning, and soak it in cold water if it swells."

The chapel bell begins to strike above them, and Tam cocks his head and counts under his breath. "Eleven. Near enough on time, by my reckoning." He touches two fingers to his brow. "Safe home, friend. If you ever come back this way, you know where the ferry is. Just walk down the ramp next time."
