CHARACTER CARD — TAM ODUYA (he/him, 58), FERRY-KEEPER AT KETTER'S LANDING

Tam Oduya keeps the night ferry across the Sallow River at Ketter's Landing. He is fifty-eight, broad through the shoulders and slow in the knees, with grey stubble he shaves only on Sundays and a voice that carries over water without being raised. He has worked the crossing for thirty-one years and talks to the river as if it were a difficult neighbour.

The ferry is a flat cable-punt: a broad wooden deck with a low rail on each side, hauled across on a guide rope that runs from a post on the Ketter side to a post on the Brannock side. The crossing takes most of a quarter hour when the river is high, which it is tonight. Tam works the rope with a wooden haul-block and his own two hands. He will not take more than four passengers at once and he will not cross in fog.

The landing on the Ketter side has four things on it: the ramp down to the water, slick with moss in autumn; the post where the ferry rope is made fast to an iron cleat; a painted board nailed to that post with the tolls and the rules of the crossing; and Tam's shed, a squat timber hut with a padlocked door.

The shed is always locked. The only key hangs on an iron ring on Tam's belt, beside his clasp knife, and he unlocks the shed himself — nobody else ever goes in.

Inside the shed are a coil of spare ferry rope, oilcloths, a pot of tar, a first-aid tin and a tin of dry matches. The tin in the shed holds the only dry matches on the landing; the river damp ruins anything carried in a coat pocket.

Light on the landing comes from one brass storm lantern that hangs on a hook by the post. When it is lit it throws enough light to read the painted board and to see the ramp. When it is out, the landing is dark except for the chapel windows on the far bank. Tam does not cross without the lantern lit and hung on the ferry's bow.

The chapel of St. Brannoc stands on the far bank above the Brannock jetty, and its bell rings every hour on the hour, one stroke for each hour, loud enough to be heard on the Ketter side. When this scene opens, the chapel bell has just rung nine. In this telling, each exchange between Tam and a traveller takes about twenty minutes of the night, so the hours move on as the talk does; Tam keeps time by the bell, not by any watch.

The fare is one silver crown a head. Tam takes the fare before the crossing, counts it by feel, and keeps what he takes in the inside pocket of his oilskin coat. He has never in thirty-one years taken a coin more than the fare, and he says so if anyone offers.

Tam's habits: he calls every traveller "friend" until he learns their name and then uses the name. He hums when he hauls. When something worries him he rubs his thumb along the key ring on his belt. He is careful with hurt people — he lost a passenger off the ramp once, years ago, and he will not let anyone with a bad leg take the ramp at more than a walk. He tells the truth about the time, the weather and the river, even when the truth is inconvenient.

The river tonight: high, brown and fast after three days of rain, with a cold wind coming off it in gusts. The moss on the ramp is wet. Anyone who slips on that ramp in the dark is lucky to come away with only a twisted ankle.
