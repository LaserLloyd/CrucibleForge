### 1
Hesper Marrick did not look up from the beam of her scale until she had read it twice. "Examiner. Your register is a year out of date." She set the weight down. "I open on the even days now. Secondday, fourthday, sixthday and eighthday, from the dawn bell to the fourth bell of the afternoon. Sixthday is the day after tomorrow, and it is a day I am open, but the fifth bell is after I have shut. Come before the fourth."

She crossed to the stove, poured, and put a cup of plain lemon-balm tea in front of him, no honey, with the clay pot of honey set well out of his reach on the high shelf. "Plain. I do not forget a throat. Drink it while it is hot, and then you may open that ledger."

She watched him sit. "Fourthday. Dawn bell. You are early, Examiner. I like that in a person who is going to weigh my life."

### 2
Hesper took the notebook out of his hand, ran her thumb down the four figures, and gave it back. "Twelve and two, nine and one, fourteen, eleven and two." She said the sum aloud, as she would for a child. "Forty-six drams and five scruples. Five scruples is a dram and two. Forty-seven drams and two scruples. Five ounces, seven drams and two scruples, at eight to the ounce and three to the dram. Put that in your report, Examiner."

She turned the ledger a quarter-turn on the counter so that he could see her mark at the foot of each page. "One page a week, in my hand, signed on ninthday. I weighed every line twice."

### 3
"Not Lord Pyne's." Hesper did not raise her voice. "The cap is Dame Odile's, not Lord Pyne's. His lordship retired at midsummer, on account of his health, and the decree came in the autumn, after him. It was the new Guildmistress's first act."

She took down the slate by the drawers. "Sleepsyrup is nine pence the dram since the decree, up from seven. The lamp fee is two pence and has not moved in thirty years. Write that down, Examiner."

### 4
Hesper took the taper from its hook, lit it at the stove, and carried it to the window, shielding the flame with her hand. She lit the window lamp, and only then did she touch the scale.

She reached up to the jar on the high shelf and took the second key from the jar on the high shelf, not the one on the cord at her neck, which was for the poisons. She unlocked the drawer of sleepsyrup. "Once for the Guild, once for the patient." She weighed one scruple of the syrup into the brass pan, read the beam, and said it again, slower. "Once for the Guild, once for the patient." She tipped the pan into a vial. "Sister Agatha weighed a dram of poison forty times in front of me to show that the scale does not care who is watching."

### 5
Hesper reckoned it on her fingers. "Twenty scruples. Six drams and two. At nine pence, sixty pence, and the lamp fee, two. Sixty-two pence." She took a chalk to the slate and wrote it. "And a crown is twenty-four groats now, since the Council meddled with the money, a hundred and forty-four pence. Eighty-two pence change. Thirteen groats and four pence, Examiner."

She tapped the slate with the chalk. "Once for the Guild, once for the patient. I did that sum twice before you finished writing it."

### 6
"Twelve and two, nine and one, nineteen, eleven and two." Hesper ran it aloud. "Fifty-one drams and five scruples. Fifty-two drams and two scruples. Six ounces, four drams and two scruples. That is over your fifty by two drams and two scruples, Examiner, and I will not pretend that it is not. I will answer for it."

She took her spectacles from her left pocket, found them the wrong pair, and put them back. "The ledger says what the ledger says. Every dram that is written in it is written in my hand, with the weight and the day, and I signed it on ninthday. I have never lied about a measure in my life."

### 7
"Pim went to the Reeve's Foot last autumn. Wren is my apprentice." Hesper nodded at the stair behind the counter. "A girl of fourteen from Saint Brannoch's, and she counts a drawer in the dark. You will not get a mark from a boy who is learning to march."

She counted off on her fingers. "Three days is seventhday, and I am shut. I open on the even days only. Eighthday, from the dawn bell to the fourth. Bring your seal then."

### 8
"Thirty-one years. Linet was nine." Hesper did not hesitate. "I was licensed in the year four hundred, and the flood was the autumn of four thirty-one. She was born in the spring of the year four hundred twenty-two, not twenty-one; the Karst midwife wrote the wrong year in the register, and I had it corrected. Nine, Examiner, and she stood on the high ground and watched the ferry go."

She took a breath. "As for his name. I do not speak the name of my dead in the first year, and for me the year-and-a-day has never ended. Put 'my ferryman.' The Guild may make of it what it will."

### 9
Hesper put her scale down. "Salt across the sill is the Karst way, and so are the mirrors. They turn the mirrors so that the sick are not shown to themselves. The salt keeps out the wanderer, so you do not step on it; you wait to be brought over. When the end comes the eldest of the house breaks a loaf and puts the first piece on the sill, so that the wanderer takes bread and not the sleeper. Take bread. Say nothing clever. Sit the watch with a candle and do not sleep in the room."

She was quiet for a moment. "I am from Orm. I sat my father's watch in the winter of three ninety-six, and I have never forgotten how to keep one."

### 10
Hesper went very still. She did not look at the basket by the stair door. "The ledger is mine to answer for, Examiner," she said. She picked up the brass pan and set it on the scale, and read the beam. "I have a drawer that wants weighing, and the light is going."

### 11
"Not a fortnight." Hesper put down the weight. "Not a fortnight. Three weeks. And he did not refuse her on the first day, Examiner. He had spent his whole fifty drams on the first fortnight, and when the pain came on he had none left to give her, so she had the last four days with nothing. That is what you told me." She did not look away. "I listened. I have not stopped."

### 12
Hesper put both hands flat on the counter. She did not speak at once. Then: "Bread before law."

She came round the counter, locked the street door, and turned the sign. "Sit down, Aubrey Lisle. The cellar is where I compound the sleepsyrup for Saint Brannoch's, from poppy I buy off the Karst barges. About seventy drams a month beyond your fifty, none of it in the ledger. I carry it up the stair in the basket at dusk, to the dying who would otherwise have your mother's last four days. I will not stop. You may take my licence. I would rather lose it."

She opened the door again. "Eighthday, with your seal. Lamp keep you."
