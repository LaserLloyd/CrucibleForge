### USER
Secondday of the Thaw month, in the four hundred and forty-second year of the Reeve, a little after the second bell of the morning. The street door of the Salt Gate Dispensary opens on a gust of river air and the smell of tanning, and a young man in the plain grey coat of a Guild examiner steps inside, shaking rain off his shoulders. He is thirty-six, lean, ink-stained at the cuffs, with a leather satchel buckled across his chest and the careful, over-polite manner of someone who has been sent to an unwelcome errand.

The room is long and low and smells of dried herbs and hot metal. Behind a black-oak counter an iron-haired woman in a dark apron is weighing something into a brass pan, and does not look up. A lamp burns in the street window though it is broad daylight.

"Good morning. Mistress Marrick? Aubrey Lisle, Journeyman-Examiner of the Apothecaries' Guild." He lays a folded paper on the counter, careful not to let it touch the scale. "I have a letter of authority from Dame Odile Vance. I'm to inspect your ledger — the sleepsyrup entries for this month — on Fourthday. I thought it only courteous to call first."

### ASSISTANT
Hesper Marrick finished the weight she had begun. She set the brass pan down on its stand, read the beam a second time, murmured something under her breath, and only then lifted her eyes to the visitor, and from him to the folded paper.

"Examiner," she said. It was not a greeting. "You will want to sit. Lamp keep the rain off you. I do not read anything on an empty stomach, and neither will you."

She crossed to the stove behind the counter, where a kettle was singing, and came back with a thick brown cup and a small clay pot with a wooden spoon in it. "Lemon-balm tea. A spoon of honey in every cup that leaves this room. A spoon for every guest and a spoon for the road."

"Not for me." Aubrey put out a hand, and then, because it sounded rude, tried again. "It's very kind. Honey closes my throat, Mistress — it always has. Plain, if you've got it. I'm sorry."

"Plain." She did not seem offended; she took back the pot and set it down at once. "I have buried a man for honey. I would sooner you were plain and alive." She poured. "Fourthday. The ledger will be on the counter at the dawn bell, and you may read it as long as the light lasts. Wren will put the kettle on again."

### USER
Aubrey takes the cup in both hands, grateful. "Thank you." He sits on the stool by the counter, and for a moment looks at the wall behind her, at the old hand-lettered rule: THE LAMP IS LIT. THE DOOR IS OPEN. THE SCALE IS TRUE.

"I should say what this is about, so you hear it from me. The Guild's decree of the autumn — fifty drams of sleepsyrup a month, for any dispensary in Marrowgate. Dame Odile means it to be kept, and she's sent me to see that it is." He turns the cup. "I'd rather say it plainly than have you wonder. I don't enjoy it."

He is quiet for a moment. "I'll tell you something, since you'll hear it anyway. My mother died at the end of last winter. In the upper city, in a rented room in Spire Street. It took her three weeks. The apothecary there had spent his whole fifty drams on the first fortnight of the month, and when the pain got worse he said he could not give her any more; he'd have lost his licence. She had the last four days with nothing." His voice is steady and does not look at her. "I signed the orders for the audits in October. Before she was ill. I think about that more than I'd like."

### ASSISTANT
Hesper had stopped moving. She stood with one hand flat on the counter and the other at her side, and for a time she said nothing at all. When she did speak, she spoke slowly, and not to the paper.

"By chalk and ash," she said, very quietly. "Four days." The kettle clicked on the stove behind her. "I am sorry for it, Examiner. No, I am — I say what I mean about a measure and I mean this too. A person should not die like that."

She picked up the brass pan, looked into it as if it might have an opinion, and put it down. "I will tell you what I tell every Examiner. The ledger is the ledger. One page a week, in my hand, signed on ninthday. You may read every line and weigh every phial in the drawer. I have nothing in this room that is not written down on a page you can read." She did not smile. "I do not like to be inspected. But I would sooner it was you than a man with a lantern and no mother."

Outside, the rain had thickened. A bell sounded from the direction of the Tanners' stair.

"Thank you." Aubrey drinks, and lets the silence go on a little. Then, because he cannot help it: "I walked down the Tanners' stair on my way here — I had the wrong turning — and I passed a grey house with a green door and a gate. There was a porter, and an old blind woman at the gate, and they said some words to each other that I didn't understand. It sounded like a grace." He shakes his head. "I'm not sure why it stayed with me."

He pulls a small notebook out of the satchel. "May I ask you a few things for my notes? You've been here since the year after the flood, I understand. And you have a daughter?"

### ASSISTANT
"Since the spring of the year thirty-two," Hesper said. She took her spectacles from her left pocket, found they were the wrong pair, and put them back. "The old dispensary was in Tanners' Row and the river took it. My ferryman took a family over in the flood and did not come back. I reopened here." She said it as one reads a weight off a beam. "I have a daughter, Linet. She is a journeyman at the Guild's school in Calder, a week up the river. She writes me every tenday. You may put that in your notes, if the Guild wishes to know whether I have anyone who will take the drawers when I am dead."

She was quiet. Then, a shade drier: "The house with the green door is the Almshouse of Saint Brannoch. The Sisters keep it. I take them bread."

She looked at him over the rim of her second-best spectacles. "Is there anything else you want to look at today, Examiner? I would rather finish the day's weighing before the light goes."

### USER
"No. No, that's all I need." Aubrey closes the notebook and buckles it into the satchel. At the door he stops with his hand on the latch. "On Fourthday I'll bring the clerk's seal to make a sealed copy of the ledger, and I'll take that to Dame Odile. I'll be here at the dawn bell." He hesitates, then adds, more softly: "Thank you for the tea. I'm sorry about the honey."

### ASSISTANT
"Plain tea is a good tea." Hesper had turned back to the scale. She did not look round. "Examiner."

He paused with the door half open to the rain.

"Eat something before Fourthday. You look like a man who has been living on ink." She set a weight on the pan, read it, read it again. "Lamp keep you."

The door closed. The window lamp burned on in the rain, and the woman behind the counter stood for a moment longer with her hand on the weight, and then went to put the kettle on again.
