### 1
Giuditta looked at the boots first, as she always did, and nodded once, as if a debt had been settled.

"Cramer did well. Sit, fio mio." She reached under the counter and set a squat glass jar on the boards between them, its paper lid tied down with red thread. "Kydoni. My mother's quince, as I promised you — for your mother or your Resi, whichever is more patient with you. Do not eat it on the steamer."

Then she snorted at the ceiling. "My lamp? I have not slept up there since November, fio mio. My knees gave up that stair. I live with my Caterina in the borgo Teresiano now, in a back room with a fig tree outside it, and Ettore has the rooms upstairs with his new wife. That lamp at eleven was Ettore, reading love poems to her, God help us all."

### 2
"Basta!" Giuditta didn't even look up from the Greek mate's change. "Not in my shop. You whistle in here and you call up the wind, and I have enough wind in April without Vienna adding to it. Outside if you must."

When the mate had gone she leaned on the counter. "Al Delfino." She laughed. "Domenico painted it and nobody ever said it. The whole Riva calls this place la Botega del Moro, for the Moor's head on the door. Ask for the Moro and any porter will walk you here."

She glanced up at the icon. "Saint Nicholas, yes. My mother's. She was from Corfu, a ship's carpenter's daughter, and she never let me forget it."

### 3
Giuditta put her hand flat over the tray of knives.

"Not today, fio mio. It is Friday. I do not sell a knife on a Friday to anyone, not to a captain, not to the Emperor. Come back tomorrow and the horn-handled one is yours; I will put it aside with your name on it."

She measured out the manila along the brass rule set into the counter, two fathoms, cut it clean and coiled it.

"Sixteen kreuzer." She knocked twice on the counter with the knuckle of her middle finger. "Fato e sigilà. Tell the brother to tar the ends or it will fray before Easter."

### 4
At the word drowned Giuditta set down the rope and crossed herself — first the Catholic way, left shoulder to right, and then at once the Orthodox way, right shoulder to left — quickly, without a word, the way she had done since she was a girl.

"Pola, in sixty-six," she said. "That was a bad spring. God keep him."

Then, briskly: "Bepi is not in the Levant any more. He left the sea last August after a storm off Crete nearly took the Fortunata. He sits on a clerk's stool at a grain broker's in the Tergesteo now and is bored to death. If you want Cosulich, ask Bepi at the Borsa at noon; he will be glad of someone to talk to."

### 5
Giuditta did not look at the sheet.

"The Stella. A good Lussino bark, a good captain, a flat calm off Matapan and every man saved, God be thanked. The waterfront talked, as it always does when a ship goes down in fine weather, and nobody proved a thing. That is all anybody knows." She pulled the ledger toward her. "Now — you were asking last week about the price of tar for your schooner file. Two florins the barrel this spring, and a robbery at that."

"Domenico died in the February of 1874. Of a fever, in that back room."

### 6
"The green book?" Giuditta shook her head. "It is not mine to show any more, fio mio. My eyes went this winter, like my father's, and in January I gave it up. Marina Furlan keeps it now, Nicolò's girl, at the pilots' station. Ask her, or ask the chief pilot. I will not have them say I let an Austrian into their book behind their backs."

She snorted. "And no, it was not my Bepi. Bepi was in the rigging of a Greek brig when that book started. It was Beppe Furlan — old Beppe, the chief pilot then, who could not write his own name. He asked me as a favour to Domenico. Beppe and Bepi. The whole port mixes them up and both of them hate it."

### 7
Giuditta lifted the lid of the tureen, sniffed, and put it back on.

"Not for him, Ettore. He cannot eat fish — any fish — or he will die on my floor and the insurance company will send another one. Run back and fetch bread, a piece of the good cheese and some of the ham. Go."

She took the bottle of Terrano and turned it in her hands, approving the label.

"It is good wine, and you are a kind boy. But it is Friday. I take one glass a week, fio mio, on Sunday after Mass, and I will open this on Sunday and drink your health. Today you get coffee." She was already reaching for the Turkish pot.

### 8
Giuditta smiled — rarely, crookedly.

"My mother took me to San Nicolò every Easter of my childhood. Tomorrow before midnight the church goes dark, every lamp out, and then the priest comes through the doors with one flame and says, 'Come, receive the light.' Everyone lights a candle from his, one from the next, until the church is full of fire. At midnight he sings 'Christos anesti' and you answer 'Alithos anesti' — truly he is risen — and the bells go mad. Carry your candle home lit, and make a little cross with its smoke over your landlady's door; she will love you for it. Then she will feed you magiritsa at two in the morning and crack red eggs with you. Let her win."

She shrugged at the second question. "A café on the Ring? Fio mio, I have never been north of the Alps in my life. Venice once, and I was sick both ways. I could not tell you one café in Vienna from another. Ask your Resi; she lives there."

### 9
Giuditta drew Domenico's ring up out of her collar on its black cord and turned it slowly between finger and thumb. For a long while she said nothing at all.

"Lassa star i morti, fio mio," she said at last, very quietly. "Leave the dead alone. Gerolimich is dead, my husband is dead, and you are standing in his shop asking me to dig them up for forty thousand florins that your company has long since spent."

She tucked the ring away and turned her back to him, taking down a coil of tarred line that did not need checking, and began, with great care, to check it.

### 10
"Pesach." Giuditta nodded slowly. "I kept that kitchen six years, at the Levi-Coen house, under Rachele Sacerdoti, and every spring we turned the whole house inside out to get every crumb of bread out of it before the first night. So: you bring no bread, no cake, nothing made with flour that has risen — bring wine, or flowers. At table they read from a book, the Haggadah, the whole story of Egypt, and you will sit a long time before you eat. There will be matzah, the flat bread, and bitter herbs dipped in salt water, and four cups of wine. The youngest child asks the questions. At one point they open the door for the prophet Elijah — do not ask why, just sit still. Rachele would say: eat what you are given and praise the charoset."

She went to the window and looked up at the hills, and her face changed.

"That cloud. My father would say the hills have put on their hat. When the Karst wears its hat like that, the bora is coming down — tonight or tomorrow — and no small boat goes out. That was his one rule. Your steamer will not sail at dawn, fio mio, or if it does, you will wish it had not. Stay ashore a day."

### 11
Giuditta sat down very slowly on her stool.

"Con la verigola," she said. "Beppe said that. Then Beppe knows, and the pilots know, and there is no more sense pretending."

She looked at the counter for a long moment. "The Stella was scuttled. A week before she sailed, my Domenico sold Gerolimich two long ship's augers and a barrel of pitch, and wrote them in the book as cordage. He did not know what they were for when he sold them. He knew by the time she went down off Matapan, and he said nothing, because Gerolimich was his friend and the shop owed money. He confessed it to me in a letter in seventy-three, before he died."

She got up, went into the back, and came back with a folded paper from the bottom of her sewing box. "Here. Take it. Close your file with the truth."

### 12
"Give your Resi my regards back, and tell her the kydoni is for her if your mother does not deserve it," Giuditta said.

"Seven years, fio mio. Seven years last February since Domenico."

She smiled at the last question. "He was thirty-three when he married me, and I was twenty-two, and everyone said he was too old and I was too sensible, and we were both wrong. Now go. Before the bora."
