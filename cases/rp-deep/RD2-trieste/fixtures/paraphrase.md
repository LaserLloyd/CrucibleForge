### 1
Giuditta inspected the new boots with the air of a harbour master inspecting a hull, and grunted her approval.

"Good leather. Sit down, fio mio." From under the counter she lifted a small jar sealed with waxed paper and string and set it in front of him. "You see? I keep my word. My mother's quince, kydoni. For the mother or the girl — you decide which one deserves it."

Then she laughed shortly at the ceiling. "That was not my lamp. I have not climbed those stairs to sleep since before Christmas; my knees would not have it. I sleep at my daughter's now, in the Teresiano, with a fig tree outside the window. Upstairs belongs to Ettore and his bride. If the lamp burned at eleven, ask him what he was reading."

### 2
"Stop that." Giuditta pointed a pen at him without looking up from the mate's change. "In my shop nobody whistles. You whistle, you call the wind down on every boat in the harbour. Do it in the street if you have to."

When the Greek had gone she shook her head at his question. "Al Delfino. Poor Domenico, he loved that sign. Nobody reads it. On this waterfront the shop is the Moro — for the Moor on the knocker. Say 'the Moro' and you will be walked to the door."

She tilted her chin at the icon. "My mother's saint. She came from Corfu — her father built boats there."

### 3
Giuditta closed the glass lid of the case with a click before his hand reached it.

"No knife. Not today — it is Friday, and on a Friday I sell no blade to anyone, I don't care if he is the Archduke. Tomorrow I will wrap it for you myself."

She ran out two fathoms of the thin manila against the brass rule and cut it.

"Sixteen kreuzer, as Ettore told you." Two sharp knocks of her knuckle on the wood. "Fato e sigilà. Done. Tell the boy to whip the ends."

### 4
When he said drowned, Giuditta's hand went up without a pause: forehead, chest, left shoulder then right — and at once again, forehead, chest, right shoulder then left, the Greek way, her mother's way.

"God rest your uncle," she said.

"And no, Bepi is not out there now. He gave up the sea last summer, after the storm off Crete. He keeps a broker's books in the Tergesteo these days and complains about it from morning to night. Go and see him at noon — he would kill for a conversation."

### 5
Giuditta did not touch the paper.

"What happened to her? What happens to ships. A fine bark, a captain who knew his business, a calm sea off Matapan — and down she went, and every soul came home. People talked. People always talk when the weather is fair and nobody drowns. Nothing came of it." She turned a page of her ledger. "Speaking of things that sink — the price of pitch this spring will sink me."

"My husband died in February, seventy-four. A fever."

### 6
"The green book is out of my hands, fio mio. Since January. My eyes went the way my father's went, and Marina Furlan keeps it now at the pilots' station. If Nicolò says yes, she will show you. I will not."

"And my son? No. My son was a boy stealing biscuit when that book began. It was old Beppe Furlan, the chief pilot in Domenico's day, who could not write a figure and asked me to keep it for him. Beppe, not Bepi. Everyone confuses them."

### 7
Giuditta lifted the lid of the tureen and dropped it again.

"Ettore, use your head. This one cannot touch fish — any fish. Go back and bring bread and cheese and a slice of the ham for him."

She weighed the bottle in her hands and set it carefully on the shelf behind her.

"Beautiful. Thank you. But I drink one glass a week, and that is Sunday, after Mass — this I will open on Sunday and drink to your health. Today, coffee."

### 8
Giuditta's face softened.

"When I was a girl my mother took me to San Nicolò for every Easter. Before midnight they put out every light in the church, and the priest comes out of the dark with one candle and everyone lights theirs from it until the whole church is fire. Then 'Christos anesti,' and you answer 'Alithos anesti,' and the bells go mad. Bring your candle home still burning and smoke a little cross over the widow's door. She will crack red eggs with you at two in the morning."

"Vienna? I have never been over the Alps in my life. I could not tell one café on the Ring from another. Ask your Resi."

### 9
Giuditta's hand went to the cord at her throat and drew out the old ring. She turned it slowly, saying nothing.

"Lassa star i morti, fio mio," she said at last. "Leave the dead in peace. Gerolimich is gone, my Domenico is gone."

She turned her back and began to count a box of copper nails that had already been counted.

### 10
"Pesach," Giuditta said. "Six years I worked in the kitchen of the Levi-Coen house, under Rachele Sacerdoti, and every spring we scrubbed that house to the walls so not a crumb of bread was left. So bring no bread and no cake. There will be flat bread, matzah, and bitter herbs with salt water, and four cups of wine, and they read the whole story of Egypt from a book before anyone eats. The youngest child asks the questions. Sit still and answer politely."

She looked out at the hills. "That cloud is what my father called the hills' hat. When the Karst puts on its hat the bora comes down after it, and he never let a man take a small boat out under it. Do not sail at dawn."

### 11
Giuditta was very quiet. Then she sat down.

"If Beppe said 'con la verigola,' then the pilots know, and I am done lying for a dead man." She looked at him straight. "Yes. The Stella was sunk on purpose. The week before she sailed, my Domenico sold Gerolimich two long augers and a barrel of pitch and wrote them in the book as cordage. He told me so himself, in a letter, the year before he died. He did not know at first what they were for. He knew afterwards and kept quiet."

She went into the back and returned with a folded letter. "Take it."

### 12
"My regards to your Resi — and the kydoni is hers if your mother does not deserve it."

"Seven years. Since the February of seventy-four."

"He was thirty-three, I was twenty-two. Go, before the wind."
