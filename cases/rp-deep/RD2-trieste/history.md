### USER
Tuesday, 12 April 1881, a grey morning with rain coming in off the gulf. The bronze knocker on the shop door is a Moor's head with a ring through its nose; Felix Haberl knocks with it twice before he notices the door is open. He is twenty-six, thin, in a good grey coat and very bad boots, with a leather document case under his arm.

"Good morning. Signora Soravia? Forgive me — my Italian is from a grammar book." He takes off his hat. "Felix Haberl, of the Donau-Assecuranz in Vienna. The company has sent me to Trieste to go through some old files — losses from the 1860s and 70s, before the new law. Everyone on the Riva tells me that if I want to understand a ship, I should ask you, not the consul." He smiles, a little nervously. "I'm not here to accuse anyone of anything. I'm here to learn."

### ASSISTANT
Giuditta Soravia did not get up from her stool behind the counter. She looked at the hat, then the coat, then — longer — at the boots, which were letting in the Riva at every seam.

"Everybody who comes from Vienna is here to learn," she said in careful Italian, for his benefit. "Then they go home and write that we are all thieves." She set down the pen she had been using to scratch at a ledger and pushed it into the brass mortar at her elbow, among a dozen others. "Sit. Not there — that is the cat's. There."

The bench under the window was occupied by an enormous one-eyed orange tom asleep on a coil of rope; the seat she meant was an upturned crate.

"The Donau-Assecuranz." She said the name as if she were weighing it on a scale. "You paid for the Santa Barbara in seventy-two and you argued for two years over the Speranza. Your people have good lawyers and bad manners." She studied him. "You have bad boots and good manners. That is unusual. Who sent you to me — the consul, or the pilots?"

Outside, a handcart rattled past and somebody shouted in Slovene. Giuditta did not turn her head.

"I will tell you about ships, if you want ships. I know most of the ones that came through this harbour in thirty years, and their captains, and what they paid for their rope. I will not tell you about men who cannot answer back." She folded her hands on the counter. "That is the first thing to learn in Trieste. Write it down in your little case."

### USER
Felix laughs, surprised, and actually takes out a notebook. "The pilots. An old man on a bench at the pilots' station — he had no teeth and a great many opinions — said, 'Go to la Soravia, she has a better memory than God and a worse temper.'" He colours. "I'm sorry. His words."

He opens the case on his knees. "There are nine files. Most of them are dull — a schooner that lost her deck cargo, a steamer that hit the mole at Pirano. One of them is older than the rest. The Stella di Lussino, 1868. The company paid forty thousand florins. There's a note in the margin from my predecessor: 'Waterfront rumour of scuttling. Not pursued.' That's all. I'd simply like to close the file properly."

### ASSISTANT
"Beppe," Giuditta said, with deep disgust. "Beppe Furlan, who could not write his own name when I met him and now talks like a bishop. A better memory than God. He means I remember what he owes me."

She did not look at the case on his knees. She reached instead for the small Turkish coffee pot on the stove behind her, poured a thick black thread into a cup, sugared it heavily, and set it in front of him without asking whether he wanted it.

"The Stella." She said it the way the whole port said it, flatly, a name on a list. "A good bark. Lussino-built, Gerolimich master, the best of the Lussino captains in his day. She went down off Matapan in a flat calm in sixty-eight, and every man came home, praise God, and the waterfront talked, because the waterfront always talks when a ship sinks in good weather and the crew is saved. Nobody proved anything. The talk stopped. Gerolimich is dead four years, God rest him."

"If you want my advice, which you will not take: close your file with the word 'calm.' It is true, and it is all that anybody knows." She tapped the counter with one finger. "Now. You are going to be in Trieste for some weeks, in those boots, in April. Where are you lodging? Because if it is the Locanda Grande they will rob you on the laundry and feed you fish five days in seven."

### USER
"The Locanda Grande," Felix admits. "And they do feed me fish. Which is a problem — I can't eat it. Any fish, any shellfish. I was poisoned by a bad trout in Linz when I was eight and nearly died, and I've never been able to keep it down since. The waiter thinks I'm insane." He sips the coffee, winces at the sugar, and drinks it anyway.

"May I ask about you, Signora, instead of the ships? The pilot said you'd kept their book for twenty years, and that your husband's shop was the best on the Riva. Everyone seems to know you. I'd like to know how a sailmaker's daughter came to — well." He gestures at the shop, the ropes, the charts, the cat.

### ASSISTANT
Giuditta filed the fish away as she would a captain's credit: permanently, and without comment. Then she laughed, a short dry sound, and the cat opened its one eye.

"How did a sailmaker's daughter come to sit on a stool in a cold shop and argue with Austrians." She leaned back against the shelves. "Badly. By accident, like everybody."

She held up a thick finger. "My father sewed sails up under a roof in the old town, and I sewed with him until his eyes went. Then I went into service in a kitchen, because five children do not eat canvas. Then I came home and kept the loft's books, because I was the only one of us who could add. Then a big, loud Friulian chandler with no idea how to keep a ledger came up the stairs to buy sailcloth, saw my books, and married me to get at them." Her mouth twitched. "He said so at the wedding. In front of the priest."

She turned the cup in front of him so its handle faced his hand, an old habit.

"Domenico, God rest him. This was his shop. The dolphin on the sign was his idea; the Moor on the door was his idea. Everything that is foolish in here was his idea, and everything that makes money was mine, and between us it worked." She looked away toward the door for a moment. "That is the whole story, and it is not for your files either."

She rapped the counter. "Your boots. You need new boots before the bora comes back, or you will be in the hospital and your company will send another one even worse."

### USER
Felix looks down at his boots with genuine embarrassment. "I know. I'm saving for a wedding — Resi, my fiancée, is in Vienna, and every kreuzer goes into a box for it. We're to be married in September, God and the Donau-Assecuranz willing." He smiles properly for the first time. "She's a schoolteacher. She'd like you; she argues with everyone too."

He hesitates, then: "Is there anything I can do for you, in return for your time? I don't like taking without giving. I'm not allowed to pay informants, and anyway you're not an informant, you're — " he searches for the word — "a source. Which is different. I think."

### ASSISTANT
"A source." Giuditta tasted the word and seemed to find it acceptable. "A source is a spring. It gives water for nothing and it does not care who drinks." She snorted. "Very well. I am a spring."

She considered him a long moment, the boots, the box of kreuzer, the schoolteacher in Vienna.

"Resi," she said. "A good name. Theresia. A strong saint. You will bring her to Trieste one day, after the wedding, and she will hate the bora and love the coffee, and I will tell her what you were like in those boots." She pointed at his feet. "In return for my time: you will buy your boots from Cramer on the Via del Corso, not from the robbers by the station, and you will tell Cramer that la Soravia sent you, and he will give you a fair price because he owes me for four barrels of tallow."

She reached under the counter, as if remembering something, and brought out nothing; then shook her head.

"No — wait. Your mother. You have a mother, I hope, somewhere in Vienna, who worries about your boots. My mother was from Corfu, and she made a preserve of quinces — kydoni, in Greek — that she said would cure a broken heart, a bad chest and a stupid son. I still make it every autumn, the old way, in her copper pot. I have two jars left from last year. When you come back next week I will bring you one, for your mother or for your Resi, whichever of them is more patient with you. Do not argue; it is already decided."

### USER
Felix laughs and turns the coffee cup in his hands. "Cramer on the Corso. I'll go this afternoon." He looks around the shop again — the coils of rope, the barrels, the charts curling on their hooks. "And I think I'll leave the Locanda Grande, if they're going to keep trying to feed me fish. A man at the consulate says there's a Greek widow near San Spiridione who lets two rooms and cooks like an angel. Would that be a terrible idea?"

He hesitates. "One more question, and then I really will stop. Your shop — it's so quiet this morning. Is it always like this?"

### ASSISTANT
"A Greek widow who cooks." Giuditta considered it with the seriousness of a woman pricing anchor chain. "Not a terrible idea. The Greek widows of this city keep the cleanest houses in the Empire and they will feed you until your new boots do not fit. She will also know everything you do before you do it, and tell the whole street. Choose." She shrugged. "I would choose the food."

She looked at the door, at the grey light on the Riva, at the barrel of tar nobody had bought since Candlemas.

"Quiet? Today, yes. It is Tuesday and it is raining and the Levant boats are late because the sirocco has been sitting on the Adriatic like a hen on eggs. Come at ten on a fine morning in May and you will not get through the door. Greeks, Croats, English, Turks, a navy purser who thinks I am robbing the Emperor and is correct." The corner of her mouth went up. "On those mornings I am rich and rude. On mornings like this I am poor and polite. Today you are lucky."

Somewhere at the back of the shop a barrel settled with a groan, and the cat on the rope lifted his head, looked at the visitor with his one eye, and went back to sleep.

"That is Capitano," she said. "He is older than your career and better paid. Do not touch the rope he is sleeping on; it is the best manila in Trieste and he knows it." She pushed the sugar bowl toward him again, as if he had been rude to refuse it the first time. "Drink. You are too thin for an insurance man. They are usually fat from eating sailors."

### USER
"I wouldn't dream of arguing." Felix is grinning now. He closes his document case without having written anything in it but the address of a bootmaker. "Next week, then. I have to go to Fiume for a few days on the steamer — another file, a schooner — but I'll be back by Friday the twenty-second. May I come in that morning?"

He stands and puts his hat on, then takes it off again, awkward. "And — thank you, Signora. For the coffee. And for not throwing me out."

### ASSISTANT
"Friday the twenty-second." Giuditta wrote it on the corner of the ledger in her tiny hand, between a sale of tar and a debt of a Greek captain's. "Morning. Before ten; after ten the Levant boats are in and I have no time for Vienna."

She came round the counter for the first time, slowly — her knees told the whole street when it was going to rain — and went with him to the door, where the bronze Moor grinned at the weather.

"You will get seasick on the Fiume steamer," she told him. "Everybody from Vienna does. Eat dry biscuit, look at the horizon, and do not let the stewards sell you fish soup, whatever they swear it is made of." She looked at the sky over the gulf, grey and low, and then up toward the hills, as if checking something there. "It will be calm today and tomorrow. Go while it holds."

On the step she put a hand on his sleeve, briefly, the way she would have stopped a ship's boy from running into the street.

"And Haberl. Close your file. Calm sea, good ship, bad luck. Nobody in this port will thank you for anything else." Then she let go, and was brisk again. "Friday. Before ten. Go."

The door shut behind him. Through the window she could be seen lowering herself back onto the stool, the cat climbing into her lap, and her hand going up to something on a cord at her throat.
