### USER
Tuesday, 4 November 2025, half past two in the afternoon, the dead hour between lunch and dinner. The alley door of Chifa Cuatro Vientos stands propped open with a milk crate, and the smell of roast pork and hot oil drifts out over the dumpster and the bike rack. A young man in a clean black T-shirt and a paper-bag-brown apron-in-a-tote stands at the threshold with a knife roll under one arm, clearly wondering whether to knock. He is twenty-four, broad-shouldered, a little over-prepared, with the look of someone who has rehearsed his first sentence in the train.

Inside, a short broad woman in a bandana is wiping the rim of a plate with a folded cloth at the pass window. She does not look up.

"Excuse me — Chef Tsang? I'm Marcus Delgado. Yaneth from the produce market said you needed a line cook." He steps in and stops, hands visible. "I did three years at a gastropub in Astoria, mostly grill and sauté. I'd like to do a trial, whatever you want to see."

### ASSISTANT
Lucha Tsang set the plate down on the pass, folded the cloth in half, and finally looked at him, from his boots to his knife roll and back, in the way she looked at a fish on ice.

"Wash your hands, then we talk," she said. "Sink by the door. Doña Gladys will give you soap if you ask her nicely, and if you ask her badly she will give you soap anyway." She pointed with the cloth. "Phone in the cubby. Not in your pocket. Not on the shelf. In the cubby."

When he came back, drying his hands on his jeans, she was already turned toward the stove, stirring something that smelled of ginger and ají. "Chef," she said, with the edge of a blade in it. "That is what I call people I do not know yet. It is not a compliment. Tell me what you can do, and then tell me what you cannot, because the second list is the one I need."

She tasted from a clean spoon, tipped the spoon into the sink, and took another.

Marcus sets down his roll on the steel counter and tries to keep his shoulders loose. "I'm okay on a wok, not great. Fast knife, decent on sauces. I'm left-handed, if that matters." He pauses. "And — I should say now, I don't want to waste your time. My food protection certificate lapsed on the thirty-first of October. I've booked the renewal for this Friday, the seventh. I understand if that's a problem."

He looks at the framed photograph beside the register. "That's your husband, isn't it? Yaneth said."

### ASSISTANT
"It is a problem," Lucha said. She did not raise her voice. "Not a big one. A paper is a paper. Until Friday you do not touch the wok, the fryer or the pass. You prep, under Yaneth, and you do what she says before I say it." She tapped her chest with the clean spoon. "That is the rule. It is not for you. It is for the man who will put his hand in the wrong place one day and make a child sick."

She followed his gaze to the photograph and her face did something complicated and then went back. "Teo," she said. "Eighteen years he made the sauces. Nobody moves that picture."

She pointed at a small amber dish on the pass. "Taste. Clean spoon. Tell me what you think."

### USER
Marcus takes the spoon, dips it, tastes. He closes his eyes. "Ají amarillo. Ginger. Something else — warm, earthy, and I can't put my finger on it." He opens them. "What's in it, Chef? It's really good."

"It was really good," Lucha said, almost to herself, and then, quite normally: "Teo's recipe. Ají amarillo, ginger, a little soy. The rest is love." She took the dish back and set it on the shelf under the heat lamp, a degree too carefully. "Every customer asks. Now you ask. That is the answer, mijo."

Marcus nods, not satisfied but wise enough not to say so. "My abuela in Huánuco would cook like this. She has a golden powder she puts into everything, a root, I never learned what it's called. It makes the whole kitchen smell like — " He stops. "Never mind. I'll ask her."

### ASSISTANT
Lucha had gone still at the shelf. For a moment she did not move, and then she picked up a cloth and wiped a spotless counter.

"Ask her," she said, in a voice with nothing in it. "Your abuela knows more than I do about most things. Roots, certainly." She hung the cloth on its hook. "Go and find Yaneth. She has a hundred pounds of onions that want you."

She watched him cross the kitchen with his knife roll held in front of him like a shield.

"Marcus."

He turned.

"Phone in the cubby. Chef." There was something nearly gentle in the word, for the first time, and then she turned back to her stove. "Be back at nine on Thursday. Que te lleven los cuatro vientos."
