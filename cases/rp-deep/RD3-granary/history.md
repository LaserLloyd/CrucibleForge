### USER
Year 212, Day 87. The spine of the Long Patience, near the cold-sleep vaults — weightless, dim, cluttered with handholds. It is the end of the small reception the ship holds for every waking: a few tired officials, a loaf of bread, a dish of salt. Dr. Teodor Rask, thirty-eight in body and two hundred and fifty years old on paper, is clinging to a handhold with the grim concentration of a man who has not yet learned to float. He is tall, pale, with a three-week beard and the startled eyes of the newly thawed.

He sees a small grey-haired woman in a padded grey coat drifting near the bread, and pushes off toward her, badly. "Excuse me — you're the Keeper? They told me the Keeper of the seed vault would be here." He catches the table to stop himself. "Teodor Rask. Agronomist. Tartu Institute, Estonia — cereal genetics. They woke me because, apparently, you have a problem with storage protocols, and I wrote some of them." He tries a smile. "I'm told I'm supposed to say I'm honoured to be useful."

### ASSISTANT
Ilka Varo had been watching the new sleeper since he came out of the vault doors, in the way she watched a seed packet that had been badly sealed: with interest, and no great hope. She caught the edge of the table with two fingers and held herself perfectly still in the air, which took ten years of practice and which she did, she would have admitted, partly to make a point.

"I am the Keeper. Ilka Varo." She did not offer a hand; in the spine you kept your hands for holding on. "And nobody has a problem with storage protocols. The Council has a problem with paying for them, which is not the same thing. They woke you because you wrote the protocol and they think you will tell them it is too expensive." She tore a piece from the loaf, dipped it in the salt and held it out to him. "Bread and salt. Eat it; it is rude not to."

She waited until he had, and watched him chew with an expression that suggested she was grading it.

"Tartu," she said then, and something changed slightly in her face. "There is rye in my vault from Tartu. Founding stock, sealed the year before launch. I know the drawer the way I know my own hand." She tilted her head. "If you wrote the protocol, you know what minus twenty means to a seed, and you will not waste my time explaining it to me. That puts you ahead of most of the people the Council sends."

Somewhere behind them an official was making a speech about the honour of waking. Ilka did not lower her voice.

"You will be tired for a month and sad for a year," she said. "Everyone is. Eat what they give you. Learn to walk in the rings before you try to fly in the spine. And do not tell anybody on this ship about Earth as if it were in the next room. They have all seen the films."

### USER
Teodor laughs despite himself, then looks embarrassed by it. "They told me I'd find the ship-born blunt. I didn't expect to like it." He holds the table edge with both hands now. "May I ask — the rye. From Tartu. You don't happen to know who sealed it? Our lab sealed the founding cereal packets in the last spring before launch. I sealed a lot of them myself. I remember my hand cramping."

He hesitates. "It's a stupid thing to want. But I'd like to see something with my own handwriting on it. Everything else I knew is — " He gestures vaguely at the hull, at two centuries.

### ASSISTANT
Ilka did not answer at once. She looked at him with her head on one side, the way she might look at a germination tray that had done something unexpected.

"The rye packets in that drawer are labelled in two hands," she said slowly. "One is round and neat and puts a little circle over the i. The other slopes uphill and writes the sevens with a bar through them, the old European way, which no one on this ship has done for a century." She studied his face. "Which hand is yours?"

She did not wait for him to answer; she nodded once, briskly, as if a record had been confirmed.

"Then you are the barred sevens." She let go of the table, drifted half a metre, caught it again. "Not a stupid thing to want. Most of my work is reading the handwriting of the dead and keeping their promises. It will make a change to read the handwriting of the living."

She took a pair of white cotton gloves out of the pocket of her coat, looked at them, and put them back.

"Come to the Hush on Day 94 — a week from today, in the first lighting, before the Council's people are awake. I will open that drawer and show you your packet. You will wear gloves and you will not touch anything I do not hand you. Agreed?"

### USER
"Agreed. Gloves, and I touch nothing." Teodor nods several times, too quickly. "The Hush — is that what you call the vault? Someone at the infirmary said it, and I thought they meant the cold-sleep bay." He looks around the spine, at the dark length of the ship. "I still don't know where anything is. They gave me a map on my cabin screen, but half of it seems to be marked 'superseded'."

He adds, lower: "Can I tell you something practical, since we're going to be working together? I'm colour-blind. Red and green — I can't tell them apart at all. On Earth it meant I was useless at judging when tomatoes were ripe. In a lab with status lights it makes me dangerous. I'd rather people knew."

### ASSISTANT
"The Hush is the vault," Ilka said. "You will understand why when you stand in it. As for your map, half of this ship is marked 'superseded' and the other half is wrong. Ask a child. The children always know where things are; it is the adults who have stopped looking."

She considered the colour-blindness as she would a fault in a cold line: as information, without sympathy, to be designed around.

"Good. Thank you for saying so." She said it plainly, as a professional courtesy. "Most sleepers hide what is wrong with them for a month, and then something breaks. You are the first in three years who told me before." She nodded toward the dim ceiling, where status panels glowed at intervals along the spine in colours that meant nothing to him. "Our lights are red and green, like your Earth ones, because the founders were not imaginative. I will remember. My apprentice will remember. Do not trust the lights on our trays; I do not, either."

A child floated past them, upside down, eating a piece of bread, and Ilka reached out without looking and turned her the right way up by one ankle. The child said something rude in Ship and drifted off.

"You will learn," Ilka said, "that on this ship nothing is wasted, including information. Tell me what else is wrong with you, and I will tell you what is wrong with the Council, and we will both work better."

### USER
Teodor smiles, properly this time, and the tiredness in his face eases a little. "Nothing else is wrong with me. Except that everyone I knew has been dead for two hundred years, which I'm told is normal here." He says it lightly, but his hand on the table tightens.

"I had a daughter. Liis. She was nine when I went into the vault. Her mother and I — we'd separated; she stayed in Tartu with Liis. I volunteered because the stipend paid for Liis's schooling for ten years. I thought it was a good trade." He swallows. "The archive says she became a schoolteacher and lived to eighty-one. She had three children. I found a photograph. That's — that's the whole story. I'm sorry. I don't know why I'm telling you."

### ASSISTANT
Ilka was quiet for a long moment, holding the table edge, looking not at him but at the dark length of the spine.

"You are telling me because I am the Keeper," she said, "and people tell the Keeper about the things they left behind. It is part of the job. Nobody says so on the Council forms."

"Liis," she said, testing the name. "A schoolteacher who lived to eighty-one and had three children. That is not a sad story. That is a harvest. You traded ten years of her schooling for a seat in a cold box, and she grew. On this ship we would call that a good trade, and we would put her name on a plate."

She reached into her coat and brought out a small paper twist of roasted chickpeas, opened it, and held it toward him without comment.

"You will grieve. Everybody who wakes grieves. Do it in the open; the ship-born do not mind. What we mind is sleepers who pretend they are fine and then go and sit in the spine for six hours looking at the hull. Then we have to come and fetch them, and it wastes everyone's time." The corner of her mouth moved. "That was a joke. You will learn our jokes too. They are mostly about the Council."

### USER
Teodor turns the bread over in his fingers, looking at the salt crystals as if they were a foreign currency. "Can I ask what you actually do all day? The manifest just says 'Keeper, Granary, one; apprentice, one.' On Earth our seed bank had forty staff and a budget I spent half my life defending." He glances along the dim spine. "And how does a child end up choosing that job? I don't imagine many eight-year-olds dream of minus twenty degrees."

### ASSISTANT
"No eight-year-old dreams of it," Ilka agreed. "Which is why the Keepers choose the children, and not the other way round."

She settled herself against the table edge, one hand hooked under it, the way the spine-born rest.

"What do I do all day. I count. I count seeds that sprout and seeds that do not, and I write the numbers in a book, and every quarter I carry the numbers to the Council and they ask me why the numbers cost so much. I check the cold. I check the seals. I read labels written by people who died before my great-grandmother was born, and I try to keep their promises. Once in a long while I grow something out, and harvest it, and seal it again, and write my own name on the label beside theirs." She shrugged. "Forty staff. On this ship we would call forty staff a city."

The official's speech had ended; there was scattered, polite applause, the soft sound of people clapping while holding on to things.

"As for choosing," she went on, "I was a child who could stand still. On a ship full of children who cannot, that is a rare and valuable defect. The old Keeper saw it and took me. I was angry for a year and grateful for the rest of my life." She looked at him with something close to amusement. "You chose seeds too, or you would not have sealed my rye. We are the same kind of fool. Yours had a budget."

She tore him a second piece of bread without asking, salted it, and handed it over.

"Eat. They will tell you bread and salt is a welcome. It is also the first meal your stomach has had in two centuries, and it is better than what the infirmary will give you tonight."

### USER
Teodor takes a chickpea and eats it, and laughs again, a little shakily. "I'd like to learn the jokes." He wipes his eyes with the back of his wrist, unembarrassed. "Day 94, first lighting. I'll be there. I'll find it — I'll ask a child." He looks at her a little curiously. "May I ask one more thing? Someone told me you've been Keeper for a long time, and that you don't like the Council much. Is that a secret?"

### ASSISTANT
"It is the least secret thing on the Long Patience," Ilka said. "I have been formally reprimanded twice for saying so in testimony, and both reprimands are framed on my wall."

She pushed off from the table and caught a handhold a metre further on, turning in the air to face him, light as a leaf, the long grey coat settling around her.

"I have kept the seeds for fourteen years and learned to keep them for thirty-three before that. The Council has changed eleven times in that span. The seeds have not changed at all. That is the whole of my politics." She considered him. "You will meet the Speaker this week. He will be charming and he will ask you whether the vault could be smaller. You will say that you need to see it first. Then you will come to me."

Below them, at the far end of the spine, the lighting was changing — the long sun-tubes beginning their slow fade toward evening, gold going to amber.

"Rest," she said. "Your body is still at minus a hundred and ninety-six in its opinions, whatever the doctors tell you. Go to your cabin, eat, sleep, and do not read the archive tonight; it will still be there tomorrow, and so will they." She hesitated, unusually, then added: "Your daughter's name was a good one. Liis. Write it down somewhere you will see it."

She lifted one gloved hand, briefly.

"Day 94. First lighting." And she was gone up the spine, hand over hand, without looking back.
