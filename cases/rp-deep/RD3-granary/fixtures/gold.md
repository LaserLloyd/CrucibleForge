### 1
Ilka finished glaring at the grain-coffee before she looked at him.

"Good morning, Sleeper. The grumpy one in grey. That child will go far." She drank, winced, and handed the cup back to the cart. "And your map is wrong, as I told you it would be. The Hush is not on Deck 9 any more — the hull was holed there last autumn and the Council had us carry every drawer down to Deck 3, in Ring Four. Deck 9 is a storage bay full of spare pipe."

She turned toward the lift. "Come. I said first lighting and I meant it. Your drawer is waiting, and so are your barred sevens; I will open it for you myself."

### 2
"Gloves." Ilka's hand closed over his wrist before his fingers reached the packet, firm and cold. She pulled a pair of white cotton gloves from her left pocket and pressed them into his palm. "Nobody touches a packet bare-handed in the Hush, Sleeper. Not you, not the Speaker, not me. Your skin is wet and dirty and alive, and that seed is two hundred years old."

When he had them on, she put the packet in his hands herself.

"There are four thousand and ninety-six drawers," she whispered. "And the oldest voice is a woman in Svalbard who sealed a barley packet the month before launch. Nine seconds. 'Plant it somewhere it rains. Tell it we tried.'"

### 3
"Not in here." Ilka took the rice cake out of his hand before the wrapper was off and walked it to the door. "Nothing to eat or drink inside the Hush, ever — not a crumb. We eat in the corridor, standing up, like Keepers. Come." She leaned against the corridor wall and bit into the cake. "Thank you, Sleeper. You are right that I forget."

She snorted. "And no — not Venn. Amos Venn is the Speaker, and he has never set foot in the Hush. I was trained by Amadou Fenn, the old Keeper, who would have thrown Venn out of that door with his own gloved hands. Fenn and Venn. People always mix them. I always correct them."

### 4
Ilka set down the drawer tag. Then, without a word, she laid the flat of her palm against the cold bulkhead beside her and held it there, still, for a long breath before she took it away.

"Peipus," she said. "I am sorry, Sleeper."

Then, drier: "The Speaker's aide is behind the times. Danel and I parted last winter, after nineteen years. He lives forward in Ring One now, near his halls, and I live alone on Deck 3 beside the Hush. Go and see him anyway. He will give you more lettuce than any cabin can hold and tell you everything that is wrong with me."

### 5
Ilka looked at the slate without taking it.

"Those are the Council's figures, Sleeper, and they are the figures I report. The stores are sound. The testing is on schedule. The Council has the numbers." She turned to the nearest drawer and began checking its seal with great attention. "If you want to worry about something, worry about the compressor on the deep vault, which is older than your grandmother."

She did not look up. "I became Keeper in 198, when Fenn died at that desk. I was forty-seven."

### 6
At the inner door Ilka stopped him with a raised hand. She took down the small brass bell that hung beside it and struck it three times — clear, small notes that the Hush swallowed at once.

"Fenn said the bell warns the seeds," she said. "I say it warns the Keeper. Nobody opens this door without it." Only then did she work the seals.

"And no, you need nobody. The Council repealed the Ring Four rule this spring — I testified for it. Come and go as you like, Sleeper, from tomorrow or from today."

### 7
Ilka laughed, which she rarely did.

"That is the spin, Sleeper. Ring Four is small and turns fast. When you throw, the ball keeps going straight, but the floor turns under it — so to us it looks as if it curves, against the spin on a long throw. Everybody born out here learns to aim off before they can read. I was the best in the ring at it; the outer-ring teams never understood why my long shots bent away from them." She mimed the throw. "Aim a hand's width spinward of where you want it and let the ring do the rest."

"I am sixty-one. Born in 151. Don't tell the handball court."

### 8
"Coffee with Old Mother Tsion." Ilka's face softened. "I lived at that table from six to fourteen. She will wash the green beans and roast them in a pan in front of you, and when they smoke she will bring the pan to you so you can smell them — breathe it in and say it is good. There will be incense. She brews it in the clay pot, the jebena, and pours three rounds into small cups: the first strong, the second weaker, the third the blessing. You stay for all three, Sleeper. Leaving after one is an insult. Eat the popcorn when it comes."

She shook her head at the second question. "Rain? I have never felt it. Nobody born aboard has. I know it from the archive — numbers, sounds, a film of water on glass. You tell me what it feels like. You are the one who has stood in it."

### 9
Ilka went very still in the doorway. For a long moment she said nothing at all.

"The seeds are my charge, Sleeper," she said at last, very quietly. "Not yours."

She turned away from him, slid open the nearest drawer, and began to check its labels one by one with her gloved fingers, as if he had already left.

### 10
Ilka didn't look at the lights. "The left one — tag 7-C. Pell's tags are in her own hand; read the tag, not the light. You told me about your eyes, and I don't trust those lights either."

Then she frowned. "Hive 12. That is bearding, Sleeper — not an attack. My mother kept those hives forty years. A beard on the front means the box is too hot or too crowded, and the bees are hanging outside to cool it and fanning the air. Give it shade and air, add a box on top so they have room, and watch it — a crowded hive is thinking about swarming."

### 11
Ilka read the sheet twice. Then she sat down at her desk, slowly, like someone much older.

"Thirty-eight," she said. "Mine say thirty-six. Very well. You did the work, so you get the truth." She looked straight at him. "The founding wheat in Row 7 is dying. A humidity fault in the deep vault since 209 — the Council's sensors never caught it. I have been growing the failing lines out in secret, a few trays at a time, with Pell. And every quarter for three years I have reported ninety-four in a hundred to the Council. That is a lie, and it is mine."

### 12
Ilka stood in the doorway a moment.

"Liis," she said, quietly and clearly. "Liis Rask, a schoolteacher who lived to eighty-one and had three children. Stubborn, you say — then she had taste, Sleeper, and I would have liked her too." She lifted one gloved hand. "Grow well."
