### 1
The Ninth String

The piano had not been played since the winter the canal froze. Mr. Hale told me so at the door, as if it were part of the address, and called me Wren, not Miss Adair. His daughter, Ines, had booked the visit by email and warned me he would talk. He did not talk. He led me down a hall that smelled of camphor and boiled milk, past coats nobody wore, into a front room where the upright stood against the wall like a guest who had come too early.

It was an old German upright, black, chipped at the corners, with candle brackets that had never held a candle. I lifted the lid and folded back the fall board. The felt had gone grey. Between the ninth and tenth strings from the bottom, wedged tight, was a tram ticket, pale blue, punched once, with 7:40 pencilled on the back. I left it where it was. Mr. Hale sat down in the armchair by the window and watched my hands.

### 2
Margit comes in at four with a bag of oranges and snow on her collar. Under the snow she wears a nurse's coat, and she is twenty-six. She bends and kisses the top of her grandad's white head before she takes off her gloves. He pats her hand twice and does not open his eyes.

She looks at the open piano, at the action on the table, at me. I tell her about the moths and the felt. She nods and puts the oranges in a bowl that holds three keys and a spool of thread. Every corner of this room is known to her, the way I know a piano, by touch, in the dark.

Then she sees the tram ticket in the strings, with 7:40 in pencil on its back, and her face goes still.

"Grandad, that is Oma's ticket," she says.

He does not answer. He opens his eyes, looks at the ticket, and then at his own hands on his knees.

### 3
Margit tells me the rest while I put the action back. "Oma had a recital across the city in the winter the canal froze," she says. "Seven forty, the ticket says. The trams stopped for the ice, so she walked, and she fell on the bridge. Grandad put the ticket in the piano that night, so no one could open it up and not find her."

I tune the ninth string last. Then I lift the ticket out and hold it to him, pencil side up.

He takes it in both hands. His thumbs rub the punched hole, once, twice, as if it might close. He looks at the clock above the boat's small stove. The deck rocks. He sets the ticket on the music desk and sits on the bench. His right hand finds a chord in the middle and rests there. His left hand lifts, waits, and comes down on one low string, then on the next, then on a third string above it.

The chord rings in tune, and the ticket leans on the music desk, its pencil marks toward the old man.
