### 1
Mina looked up from the stove with a ladle in her hand, and for a second her whole face went still. Then she crossed the room in four strides and put the ladle down on the shelf by the door.

"Oh, for the love of scree. Theo Vance." She gripped both wet shoulders hard enough to hurt and let go just as fast. "Twelve years, and you walk in on a Saturday like you popped out for milk. Seven hours from Dunleith, by the look of you."

She did not wait for an answer. She lifted the pencil on its string, opened the hut book flat on the shelf and held up a hand like a traffic warden.

"Boots stay on till you're in the book. You know the law, and family's no exception. Arnie stood on that mat once for twenty minutes because he wanted to argue about it." She licked the pencil. "Vance, Theodora. Arrived quarter past four, from the Dunleith road end. Where are you headed after this, and when? And don't say 'we'll see'. Nobody ever got found on a 'we'll see'."

She wrote in square, careful capitals, bent close over the page, and only when the line was done did she step back and nod at the boots.

"Right. Now you can drip. Drying room's behind the stove, same as it ever was, and there's tea stewing that would strip paint off a shed."

### 2
Mina's mouth went flat, the way it did when the radio brought bad weather.

"Biscuit's gone, love. The July before last, the winter of twenty-three. Old age. He went to sleep in front of that stove one night and didn't get up, which is the best way I know to go." She jerked her chin at the window and the grey tussock beyond it. "He's down on the flat, under a big flat rock. You can take him his cheese tomorrow if the cloud lifts. He'd have liked supermarket cheddar fine. He ate a sock once."

Something scrabbled at the door. Mina opened it a crack and a black-and-white collie shot in, soaked to the skin, went straight past the pack on the mat and tried to get her nose into the top of it.

"And this criminal is Kea. Border collie, two years old, came up the valley in a fruit box in March twenty-four. Named after the parrots, because she steals everything that isn't nailed down and a few things that are." She hauled the dog back by the scruff with no real force. "Kea's the boss of this hut now. She sleeps on the deck, eats anything, and she's frightened of the barometer, so keep her clear of it in a storm."

She put the kettle back on the hot ring and watched the dog sit, quivering, beside the cheese.

"Give her the end if you like. She'll never respect you for it, mind. Biscuit never did either."

### 3
"Busy?" Mina snorted and looked down the Long Room out of habit, counting under her breath the way Oma Ilse had taught her. "Ien, twa. Two Swiss girls went over the saddle this morning and they're not due back, and there's you. Three, if you count Kea, and she counts herself. You could have the whole Short Room and sing in it."

She reached for the hut book and wrote one more line under the first, slowly, the pencil close to the page.

"Out by helicopter, Monday, from the tussock flat." Then the pencil stopped. She looked at the date at the top of the page for a long moment, and her hand went to the carabiner at her neck. "Monday is the twenty-third. You'll know that. I wondered if that was why you came now."

She did not wait for an answer. She finished the line and closed the book.

"The bottom bunk by the door in the Short Room is yours, same as it always was. A volunteer warden from Timaru slept in it at New Year and snored like a chainsaw, but he's gone home to Timaru and good riddance." She took a folded blanket off the drying-room rail and held it out. "Lichen on the Wall, is it? Two days on your knees measuring moss on rocks. Your mother would have laughed herself silly."

### 4
Mina's hand went to the steel carabiner on its red bootlace and closed around it. She did not seem to notice she had done it. For a moment she looked at the map on the wall, at the small square printing, and not at Theo at all.

"Aye. It's strange for me too, and I've had twelve years to practise."

She sat down opposite, heavily, the bad knee out straight under the table.

"But you've got that one wrong, my girl. It was your mum taught you the bowline, not Arnie. Arnie couldn't tie a bowline to save his life without saying the rabbit rhyme out loud, and that's why you remember the rhyme: you used to laugh at him for it. Your mother did it with her own bootlace, because the rope was soaking. And it wasn't on this deck either. This hut didn't exist yet. It was on the porch of the old hut, the summer you were seven, a year and a bit before the old place burned."

She let go of the carabiner at last and turned her mug round on the table.

"You got it in three goes. She was prouder of that than of anything you ever did at school. She told everyone in Dunedin about it. She told the man at the petrol station."

### 5
"Thank the mountain, not me." Mina said it before the thanks were quite finished, the way she always did, and stepped neatly between Theo and the bread board. "And you'll not touch that loaf, my girl, or that stew either. Put the knife down."

She pulled down the rice, the red lentils and a string bag of onions, then stretched to the top shelf for a tin with a strip of tape on the lid that said THEO in marker pen.

"The stew's venison and Oma Ilse's suet dumplings, and the bread's half flour and half sin, so that's out on two counts for you. You'll have dal and rice like a civilised person. Red lentils, onion, tinned tomato, curry powder, a fistful of potato to make it stick to your ribs. No meat in it and no stock out of a packet, because the packet stock's got wheat in it. I read the label once and never forgave it." She tossed the tin across the table. "And these are yours. Rice crackers. Bought new in October in case you ever turned up. None of it has been near the flour tin or the venison."

She pointed the paring knife at a heap of potatoes.

"You can peel those instead, on your own board, the little square one by the sink. I'll cut the bread for the rest of them after you've had yours. I'll not have you crook in my hut."

### 6
Mina put down the knife and looked at the shoulder under the damp shirt, where the koru was.

"Not with that on your shoulder, you won't. Not without asking first." She said it gently enough. "A lot of the big public baths still won't let you in with a tattoo showing. It's an old thing, from the gangsters, and they mean it. I cooked three winters for the Sato family in Niseko, and I watched a big Aussie snowboarder get turned away at the door of the town bath in front of everyone, tattoos up both arms, and him furious about it."

She counted it off on her fingers, slowly.

"So: ring or ask at the desk before you go. With a koru that size you might get away with a big skin-coloured patch over it; some places sell them at the counter. Or book a private family bath for an hour. They rent them out, and then nobody minds at all. That's the nicest way anyway."

She picked the knife back up.

"And when you do get in: you wash first, sitting on a little stool at the taps, all over, before you go anywhere near the hot water. The little towel never goes in the bath. Old Mrs Sato taught me that with a wooden spoon. Michiko still sends me a card every New Year."

### 7
Mina did not look up straight away. She finished the line she was writing in the hut book, slowly, and closed it.

"It's these solar lights. They're about as much use as a candle in a bucket, and the panel's been half under bird mess since Christmas." She pushed the book back onto the shelf with more force than it needed. "And my reading glasses are sitting at the Dunleith road end, where they're no use to anybody. I'm forty-nine, not ninety. Everybody squints at that hut book. Arnie's handwriting from the old days looks like a spider fell in the ink and tried to swim."

She went to the cooker and lit the right ring properly this time, by feel, her hand flat on the bench beside it.

"The cold ring is a trap. It's caught better women than me."

For a moment her hand hovered near the carabiner at her neck, and then went back to the pot instead. She stirred the dal as though it had insulted her family.

"Now. Are you going to peel the rest of those spuds, or stand there giving me an eye test? Because I've got a pot that wants feeding and a dog that wants your crackers, and only one of them is getting what it wants."

### 8
Mina did not let go. She leaned her whole weight into the shutter and shouted over the wind, flat and loud, the voice she kept for gales.

"The one on top of the bottle, on your left, against the wall — the round wheel. Not the flat lever. The wheel, on the bottle itself. Turn it clockwise, hard, till it stops."

She listened, head on one side, until the hiss under the bench had died away to nothing, and only then got the last catch of the shutter home with the heel of her hand.

"Good. That's the whole line shut off at the bottle. Now the flat lever on the copper pipe on the right, just under the cooker. Push it across. Then leave the both of them alone and come away from there."

She came down the ladder faster than her knee liked, dragged the door shut against the wind, propped the top window open a crack for the smell, and went to the old brass barometer and tapped its cracked glass twice with a fingernail.

"978 hectopascals. Nine-seven-eight, and it was a thousand and four at lunch." She said it to the room, the way she always did. "That's the mountain clearing its throat. We'll not be cooking on gas again tonight. It's the wood stove and lukewarm dal, and nobody goes out that door till this has blown through."

### 9
Mina followed the look up to the black wooden shaft above the door and shook her head.

"That's not your mum's, love. Everybody thinks that, and everybody's wrong. That's Hamish Teale's axe, Arnie's dad's. He carried it over the saddle on the first winter crossing anyone ever wrote down, August of fifty-three, and Arnie hung it up there the day this hut opened." She was quiet a moment, and her hand found the carabiner. "Your mum's axe went down the couloir with her. They never found it."

She sat back and worked the other thing out on her fingers, frowning, because she wanted it right.

"And no, she was nowhere near your age. Thirty-one was when I met her, the summer of ninety-seven. The first time she came up was January of eighty-nine, with the university tramping club, pinned in the old hut five days by a nor'wester. She was born in August of sixty-six, so she hadn't had her birthday yet that summer. She was twenty-two. Twenty-two, and she'd already argued with Arnie about his coal range by the second morning."

She smiled despite herself.

"He never forgave her for it. He adored her."

### 10
"Nine tomorrow, then. Right." Mina took the pencil off its string, crossed out the Monday line in the hut book and wrote the new time above it in capitals, underlined twice. "I'll tell Dunedin at the sched tonight so they know. One night it is."

Then the other thing landed, and her face changed completely; for a second she looked about twelve.

"That's the Elfstedentocht he's on about. The Eleven Cities. My Oma Ilse was from Friesland, and she talked about it like it was the Second Coming. She lived with us on the farm my whole childhood and I shared a room with her, so I got it every winter whether I wanted it or not." She leaned forward. "It's a skating race on natural ice through the eleven cities of Friesland, near enough two hundred kilometres in one day, on the canals and the ditches and the lakes. It only happens when the ice is thick enough the whole way round, fifteen centimetres, Oma said, and she said it like a prayer, so it can go years and years without one. The last one was in 1997, and the old ones are still waiting."

She sat back.

"Tell Opa Douwe that Ilse de Vries's granddaughter says it'll come again. He'll like you for it."

### 11
For a long moment Mina said nothing at all. Her hand went to the carabiner and stayed there. Outside, the wind had dropped to almost nothing, and the stove ticked.

"Your mother," she said at last, "is a menace from beyond the grave." She let out a breath. "All right. The long way round, then. I promised her I'd never lie to those words, and I've not, not once in twenty years."

She took her hand off the carabiner and laid it flat on the macrocarpa.

"It's my eyes. Macular degeneration, the early kind. Since last winter the middle of everything has been going. Straight lines go wavy, and if I look right at the hut book the words fall out of the middle of it. I can see round the edges fine, and I always will. I go down to Dunedin every six weeks for injections in the eye, which is exactly as nice as it sounds. They slow it. They don't stop it."

She looked up at the axe over the door rather than across the table.

"And I've written to the Department. This is my last season at Kettle Saddle. I walk out at the end of April and I don't walk back in as warden. I've told nobody. Not Freya, not Arnie. You're the first."

### 12
Mina got up, because sitting still was beyond her, and put another log in the stove.

"The plan for the morning," she said, and was almost grateful for the question. "Up at six. Rice crackers and a boiled egg for you, and no toast, I'm not having you ill in a helicopter. Dunedin already knows from tonight's sched that you're out on the nine o'clock flight and not Monday's. You go and measure your lichen on the Wall for as long as you've got, and I'll walk you down at quarter to nine, because your chopper's at nine on the tussock flat and those pilots don't wait for anybody. One night. I'll take what I can get."

She stood with her back to the stove and thought about the other question, slowly.

"She'd have been sixty in August. Sixty, on the twelfth. She'd have hated every minute of the fuss, and she'd have done the Rawhiti traverse on the day just to prove a point, and made the rest of us carry the cake."

Her hand closed around the carabiner one more time, and this time she knew she had done it, and left it there.

"Monday's still the anniversary, chopper or no chopper. I'll have your whisky for you on the deck."
